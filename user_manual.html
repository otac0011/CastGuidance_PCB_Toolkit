Reference (12 tools)

Ohm's LawReference
When to use: any time you know two of voltage, current, resistance, or power, and need the other two.

Why it matters: Ohm's law and the power equation are the foundation of every electronics calculation. They're how you size a current-limit resistor, predict how warm a power resistor will run, decide whether a sense resistor will saturate the ADC, or check whether the ribbon cable from your bench supply can survive the load. Getting them right keeps the smoke inside the chips; getting them wrong destroys the cheapest parts in your BoM (the resistors) and sometimes a few of the most expensive (the regulators or MCUs downstream).

How it works: The four classic equations — V = I·R, P = V·I, P = I²·R, and P = V²/R — are completely linked. Any two of {V, I, R, P} mathematically determine the other two. The tool exposes all four solve modes from a single screen, and it rounds to standard E-series resistor values (E12 or E24) so the answer is something you can actually order, not a number with five decimal places that doesn't exist as a part.

What you'll enter:

  • Voltage (V) V / mV / kV — the potential across the element. Typical rails: 1.8 V (FPGA core), 3.3 V (MCU IO), 5 V (USB), 12 V (auto / desktop), 24 V (industrial), 48 V (server / PoE), 120 V or 230 V (mains).
  • Current (I) A / mA / µA — flow through the element. Typical: µA range for sleep-mode MCUs, mA for signals and LED drives, multi-A for power rails and motors.
  • Resistance (R) Ω / mΩ / kΩ / MΩ — the limiter. Typical: 1 mΩ–100 mΩ for current shunts, 100 Ω–10 kΩ for current-limiting and signal terminations, 4.7 kΩ–100 kΩ for pull-ups, >100 kΩ for ADC dividers.
  • Power (P) W / mW — heat dissipated. Surface-mount resistor ratings: 0402 ≤ 62.5 mW, 0603 ≤ 100 mW, 0805 ≤ 125 mW, 1206 ≤ 250 mW, 2010 ≤ 750 mW, 2512 ≤ 1 W.

Conversion CalculatorReference
When to use: converting between units — mils ↔ mm, inches ↔ mm, °C ↔ °F, dB ↔ ratio, complex rectangular ↔ polar form.

Why it matters: PCB datasheets mix mils, mm, inches, °C, °F, dBm, and dBV freely. American board houses still default to mils for trace width; European fabs default to mm. A single bad conversion can leave a trace 25.4× too wide. Catching unit-conversion mistakes before the design is sent to fab saves a respin.

How it works: Tabbed converter with one tab per domain — Distance (mil/inch/mm/cm/m), Temperature (°C/°F/K), Power (dB ↔ linear ratio, dBm ↔ mW, dBV ↔ Vrms), Complex (rect ↔ polar, degrees ↔ radians). Each tab is bidirectional: type the number on one side, read it on the other.

What you'll enter: any value on either side. Common conversion factors burned into your memory after using it for a week: 1 mil = 0.0254 mm, 1 inch = 25.4 mm = 1000 mils, 0 dBm = 1 mW = 224 mVrms @ 50 Ω, 10 dB = 10× power = ~3.16× voltage.

Tip: When in doubt, double-check by converting back. A round-trip mil → mm → mil should land exactly where you started.
SI Prefix ReferenceReference
When to use: reading a datasheet and you can't immediately recall what zepto, atto, ronto, or quetta mean.

Why it matters: The SI prefix system spans 60 orders of magnitude. Most engineers comfortably hold the centre ten (femto through tera), but the edges show up in real datasheets — sub-aA leakage currents on precision opamps, multi-PHz photonics. A lookup table beats Googling.

How it works: Static table with all 21 official SI prefixes from quecto (10⁻³⁰) to quetta (10³⁰), including the four 2022-additions (ronto, quecto, ronna, quetta). Each row shows name, symbol, power of ten, and a worked example.

Tip: "M" is always mega (10⁶); "m" is always milli (10⁻³); the capital-vs-lowercase distinction matters and tools that get it wrong have caused real outages.

Wire Ampacity / Resistance / ThermalReference
When to use: sizing an off-board wire — power feeders, battery interconnects, motor leads, magnet-wire windings, antenna feeds, harness wires inside an enclosure.

Why it matters: Undersized wire melts insulation, sets fires, blows out like a fuse. Oversized wire wastes weight and cost in a vehicle or aerospace application. Long runs with too small a gauge starve the load — your sensor reads wrong because the supply rail sagged on the cable. This is the single most common "what gauge should I use?" question on every electronics forum, and it has four answers depending on whether you're asking about heat, voltage drop, mechanical strength, or fault current.

How it works: Combines four physics models. (1) DC resistance per metre from the AWG table, scaled for the actual conductor material and corrected to operating temperature (R(T) = R₂₀ · [1 + α(T-20)]). (2) Maximum continuous ampacity from a heat-balance equation calibrated against the AWG chassis baseline, accounting for the conductor's own self-heating as resistance rises with temperature. (3) Preece fusing current (I_fuse = K · d^(3/2)) for the catastrophic limit. (4) Voltage drop and power dissipation at any operating current.

What you'll enter:

  • Gauge AWG 0000 – 40 — pick from the dropdown. Higher number = thinner wire.
  • Conductor material — solid or stranded copper (default for everything electronics), aluminum 1350 (utility / aerospace power), copper-clad aluminum (cheap audio), nichrome (heaters), constantan / manganin (precision resistance), tinned or silver-plated copper.
  • Insulation — 20 classes from PVC 60 °C up to Kapton 260 °C, including THHN / XLPE / XHHW (building wiring), PTFE / FEP / ETFE / PFA (aerospace), and the six IEC 60317 magnet-wire enamel classes A through R.
  • Ambient temperature °C — the air around the wire. Inside a sealed enclosure expect 20–40 °C hotter than the room.
  • Length m / mm / inch — one-way run length (the tool doesn't double it for the return path).
  • Current A — optional; 0 ⇒ just show max ampacity. Enter your operating current to also get voltage drop, power dissipated, and wire temperature.
  • Source voltage V — optional; enables voltage-drop-as-percentage output.
  • Frequency Hz — optional; enables skin-depth and AC-resistance bump for RF use.

Reactance & LC ResonanceReference
When to use: figuring out the impedance of a cap or inductor at a frequency, or where an LC tank resonates.

Why it matters: Decoupling networks, filters, oscillators, antenna matching, and EMI suppression all depend on knowing the reactance of a part at the frequency of interest. A 100 nF "bypass" cap at 100 MHz is 16 mΩ; the same cap at 10 kHz is 160 Ω — same part, four orders of magnitude difference. Resonance frequency tells you where an LC circuit ranges from being capacitive to being inductive.

How it works: Three solve modes. Xc = 1 / (2π·f·C) (capacitive reactance — drops with frequency). Xl = 2π·f·L (inductive reactance — rises with frequency). LC resonance = 1 / (2π·√(L·C)) — the frequency where Xc = Xl. Q (quality factor) is also reported when both L and a series R are entered.

What you'll enter:

  • Capacitance (C) pF / nF / µF / mF — typical: 1 pF (precision RF), 100 pF (NPO bypass), 10–100 nF (logic decoupling), 1–10 µF (bulk decoupling), 1000+ µF (electrolytic reservoir).
  • Inductance (L) pH / nH / µH / mH — typical: 0.5 nH (via parasitic), 1–10 nH (RF chip inductors), 1–10 µH (power-supply filter), 100–1000 µH (buck-converter inductor).
  • Frequency (f) Hz / kHz / MHz / GHz — the frequency you care about.
  • Series R Ω — optional, for Q. Typical: 0.5–5 mΩ for power inductors, < 100 mΩ for chip inductors at low frequency.

LED Series ResistorReference
When to use: picking a current-limiting resistor for a single LED or LED string driven from a constant-voltage source.

Why it matters: LEDs are current-driven devices with a nearly-vertical I–V curve above Vf. Without a current-limiting element, a 2.0 V LED on a 3.3 V rail draws unlimited current and instantly self-destructs. The simplest fix is a series resistor; the tool sizes it correctly and rounds to an actually-orderable E-series part.

How it works: R = (Vsupply – Vf) / I_target, then snaps to the nearest E12 or E24 value. It also computes the power dissipated in the resistor (P = I² · R) so you can pick the right package wattage.

What you'll enter:

  • Supply voltage (Vs) V — the rail you're driving from (3.3 V, 5 V, 12 V are common).
  • LED forward voltage (Vf) V — from the LED datasheet at your operating current. Typical: 1.8–2.1 V (red), 2.0–2.5 V (yellow / amber), 2.0–2.5 V (green), 2.8–3.4 V (blue / white), 3.0–3.5 V (UV / high-power).
  • Target current (I) mA — typical: 5–10 mA (indicators, low brightness), 15–25 mA (standard panel LEDs), 50–100 mA (high-brightness), 350–700 mA (1 W / 3 W power LEDs — driven from a constant-current driver, not a simple resistor).
  • Number of LEDs in series — multiplies Vf. Useful for "string of 3 white LEDs from 12 V" calculations.

Voltage DividerReference
When to use: bringing a higher voltage down to an ADC-friendly range, building a bias-voltage network, or solving for any missing element of a 2-resistor divider.

Why it matters: Two resistors form the simplest analog circuit there is. They're everywhere — battery monitor, gate bias, threshold detect, midpoint reference. Picking E-series values that hit the exact ratio you want and that don't load the source is fiddly enough that calculator help is worth it.

How it works: The classic Vout = Vin · R2 / (R1 + R2). Four solve modes — fix any two of (Vin, Vout, R1, R2), the tool computes the other two and snaps to E12/E24. Also reports the divider's current draw (I = Vin / (R1+R2)) and its output impedance (R1 || R2) so you can check it's compatible with whatever loads it.

What you'll enter:

  • Vin V — the source voltage being divided.
  • Vout V — the desired voltage at the divider's midpoint.
  • R1 Ω / kΩ — the top resistor (between Vin and midpoint).
  • R2 Ω / kΩ — the bottom resistor (between midpoint and ground).
Tip: For ADC inputs, the output impedance (R1 || R2) should be ≤ 10 kΩ for typical SAR ADCs, and ≤ 1 kΩ for fast successive-approximation. Pair the divider with a small bypass capacitor (1 nF) to ground for noise rejection.
Series / Parallel R-L-CReference
When to use: combining up to 8 resistors, capacitors, or inductors in series or parallel to hit an exact target value from your stock of standard parts.

Why it matters: You need 23.7 kΩ but standard values jump from 22 kΩ to 24 kΩ. You need 47 nF but only have 22 nF and 100 nF on hand. You need 1.7 µH but the closest stock part is 2.2 µH. This tool gets you to the target without ordering new parts.

How it works: Three modes — R, L, or C — and two topologies — series or parallel. Series: values add directly for R and L; reciprocals add for C. Parallel: reciprocals add for R and L; values add directly for C. Up to 8 elements, with E-series rounding on the input side.

What you'll enter: one row per element you want to combine — value, units, and quantity (if you have multiples of the same part). Output shows the combined value and any standard tolerance stack.

Tip: Parallel combinations of equal-value resistors heat-spread the dissipation. Two 1 kΩ 0603s in parallel give 500 Ω at twice the power rating of one part — useful when no single part hits both the resistance and the wattage spec.
PPM Frequency ToleranceReference
When to use: converting a crystal datasheet's ppm spec into actual Hz of drift, or vice-versa.

Why it matters: Communications standards have tight frequency tolerances. CAN bus and USB allow ±500 ppm. Ethernet Gigabit allows ±100 ppm. GPS receivers want a TCXO at ±0.5 to ±5 ppm. Pick a crystal with the wrong tolerance and the interface fails initialisation. Pick one too tight and you've over-spec'd by 10× — TCXOs cost an order of magnitude more than plain crystals.

How it works: Bidirectional: ppm × nominal_frequency × 10⁻⁶ = absolute drift in Hz. Type either side, get the other.

What you'll enter:

  • Nominal frequency Hz / kHz / MHz / GHz — what the crystal is supposed to oscillate at.
  • Tolerance ppm — typical: ±10 to ±30 ppm (general-purpose crystals), ±5 to ±10 ppm (oven-controlled cuts), ±0.5 to ±5 ppm (TCXOs), ±0.001 ppm (OCXOs / rubidium).
  • OR Drift in Hz — type the Hz directly and read back the ppm.

XTAL Load CapacitorReference
When to use: sizing the two load caps (C1, C2) for a Pierce oscillator around a parallel-resonant crystal.

Why it matters: A parallel-resonant crystal is specified at a load capacitance (commonly 10, 12, 18, 20, or 32 pF). The crystal will oscillate slightly off its nominal frequency unless C1 in series with C2, plus the chip's pin capacitance and the PCB stray, sums to that load. Sloppy load networks cause start-up failures, slow oscillator pull-in, ageing drift, and bad time on every device using that clock.

How it works: The crystal's actual load equation is CL = (C1 · C2) / (C1 + C2) + C_stray. The tool inverts that — given CL and C_stray, returns C1 = C2 such that the equation balances. C_stray usually consists of: pin capacitance of the MCU oscillator pins (5–7 pF each) plus PCB trace capacitance (1–3 pF total). When in doubt, use 10–12 pF total stray as a starting point.

What you'll enter:

  • Crystal CL pF — from the crystal datasheet. Common values: 8, 10, 12, 18, 20, 32 pF.
  • Stray capacitance pF — pin + PCB. Typical 8–14 pF total (5–6 pF per pin × 2 pins + 1–3 pF PCB).

Conductor Impedance (analytic)Core PCB
When to use: dialing in trace width on a controlled-impedance line — 50 Ω single-ended, 100 Ω differential, or any custom Z₀.

Why it matters: If a trace isn't the right characteristic impedance, every fast edge gets a reflection. Reflections cause ringing, overshoot, EMI, lost margin, and at high speeds outright bit errors. Every fab house's "Z-coupon calculator" and every commercial 2D field-solver lives or dies by these same analytic formulas.

How it works: Pick a topology and the tool runs the appropriate closed-form equation. Microstrip uses Hammerstad-Jensen 1980 with the Kirschning-Jansen frequency dispersion correction (industry standard, accurate to ~1 % below 10 GHz). Stripline uses Wadell 1991 with full conductor-thickness compensation. Coplanar waveguide (with or without backside ground) uses Ghione-Naldi 1987. Differential versions of all the above add a coupling correction. The math is bidirectional — solve for Z₀ given a width, or solve for width given a target Z₀ (inverse Hammerstad).

What you'll enter:

  • Topology — microstrip (outer layer, one ground below), embedded microstrip (outer layer with cover laminate), stripline (between two grounds), asymmetric stripline (different distance to each ground), CPW or GCPW (coplanar with ground rails).
  • Substrate height (h) mil / mm — vertical distance from the trace to the reference ground plane. Typical: 4 mil (4-layer outer), 6–8 mil (4-layer inner), 60 mil (2-layer 1.6 mm board).
  • Trace width (W) mil / mm — what you're solving for, or the value if going forward.
  • Copper thickness (t) mil / µm / mm — typical: 0.5 oz = 0.7 mil = 17.5 µm, 1 oz = 1.4 mil = 35 µm, 2 oz = 2.8 mil = 70 µm.
  • Substrate Dk (εᵣ) — dielectric constant at the operating frequency. FR-4: ~4.3 at 1 GHz, dropping to ~3.9 at 10 GHz. Rogers RO4350B: 3.48. Megtron 6: 3.45.
  • Operating frequency GHz — required for the dispersion correction on microstrip.
  • Target Z₀ Ω (in inverse mode) — typical: 50 Ω single-ended, 75 Ω video/CATV, 100 Ω diff per leg.

Trace Impedance (openEMS 3D)Core PCB
When to use: when the analytic formula's assumptions break — embedded microstrip, asymmetric stripline, weird coplanar geometries, soldermask-covered traces, or for an authoritative second-opinion check on a critical impedance.

Why it matters: Analytic equations work great for the geometries they were derived for. Real boards have soldermask covering the traces, edge-of-board effects, asymmetric reference planes, and via discontinuities that shift Z₀ by 2–5 % from the analytic prediction. For a tight-tolerance impedance spec (e.g. 50 Ω ± 5 %), that 2–5 % shift can put you out of tolerance — so a 3D field solver is the right answer.

How it works: Wraps the open-source openEMS FDTD (finite-difference time-domain) engine. The tool generates the 3D geometry (substrate, trace, plane, soldermask if applicable), meshes it, excites a port at one end, and measures the reflected and transmitted waves. From the phase-velocity extraction it backs out Z₀. The analytic Hammerstad/Wadell answer is computed in parallel so you can see the discrepancy.

What you'll enter:

  • Topology — same eight options as the analytic tool.
  • Material — pick from the dropdown (Megtron 6, Rogers RO4350B, FR-4 generic, etc.) — auto-loads Dk and Df. Or "Custom" for manual Dk entry.
  • Geometry dimensions — W, t, h, h_top, s, s_b, g (some hidden depending on topology).
  • Trace length — Low (300 mil) / Normal (600 mil) / High (900 mil) / Extra (1200 mil). Longer is more accurate but takes longer to simulate.
  • Convergence — −20 dB (fast, less accurate) to −50 dB (slow, more accurate). Default −50 dB.
  • Mesh fineness — Fine (0.8×) / Normal (1.0×) / Coarse (1.2×) / Extra Coarse (1.5×). Finer mesh = slower + more accurate. Default Normal.
  • Worker threads — 0 = auto-detect, or specify a number. Auto picks all cores.
Tip: Start with "Analytic Fast Calc" (no simulation) to validate the geometry inputs. Only run the 3D simulation when you've confirmed the analytic answer is in the right ballpark — a 30 s setup mistake doesn't waste 2 min of CPU. Also use "Preview 3D Geometry" to eyeball the structure before committing to a run.
Differential PairsCore PCB
When to use: sizing the trace width and spacing for any differential pair (USB, HDMI, MIPI, PCIe, Ethernet, LVDS, CAN-FD, SATA, DisplayPort).

Why it matters: Differential signaling is the dominant choice above ~250 MBd for noise rejection and reduced EMI. Every standard specifies a different Zdiff (typ. 90 Ω USB, 100 Ω Ethernet/PCIe/SATA, 100 Ω HDMI with ±15 %). If you get the spacing wrong the differential impedance is off — the eye closes, ESD margin drops, EMC scans show common-mode emissions. The "5/5 rule" (5 mil trace, 5 mil space) is almost never right.

How it works: For an edge-coupled pair the differential impedance is approximately Zdiff = 2 · Z₀ · (1 − 0.48 · exp(−0.96·s/h)), where Z₀ is the single-trace impedance and s/h is the spacing-to-height ratio. Common-mode impedance is computed by treating the pair as two parallel single-ended lines. The tool also reports the coupling coefficient k and a back-of-envelope near-end crosstalk (NEXT) estimate.

What you'll enter:

  • Topology — edge-coupled microstrip / stripline / CPW with ground, or broadside-coupled stripline (one trace stacked above the other).
  • Trace width (W) mil / mm — each leg.
  • Spacing (S) mil / mm — edge-to-edge distance between the two traces.
  • Substrate height (h) mil / mm.
  • Substrate Dk and copper thickness — same as the single-ended impedance tool.
  • Operating frequency — for the dispersion correction.

Conductor Properties (IPC-2152 / IPC-2221)Core PCB
When to use: sizing any power-carrying trace — VBus, motor drivers, regulator outputs, charger paths, ground returns.

Why it matters: Too narrow and the trace gets hot, browns the laminate, eventually fuses. Too wide and you waste real estate. IPC-2152 (2009, the modern standard) is significantly more accurate than the legacy IPC-2221 (1998) — IPC-2152 numbers run roughly 2× higher than IPC-2221 for the same temperature rise, because IPC-2221 was the original Brooks 1956 data being conservative. Pick the wrong standard and you're either over-engineering or under-engineering.

How it works: Both standards take a temperature-rise target and a current, and return the minimum trace width — or the inverse. The math is empirical: IPC-2221 uses Brooks' constants A = k · (ΔT^b) · (I^c); IPC-2152 uses revised constants from a 2009 measurement campaign. External (outer-layer) traces run cooler than internal (between planes) traces because they have air on one side.

What you'll enter:

  • Methodology — IPC-2152 (recommended for new designs) or IPC-2221 (legacy / conservative).
  • Layer location — External (outer copper) or Internal (between dielectric layers, no air cooling).

Fusing Current (Onderdonk)Core PCB
When to use: finding the fault-current that would melt a PCB trace, or designing a deliberate sacrificial trace as a one-time fuse.

How it works: Onderdonk's equation, derived from thermodynamic energy balance assuming adiabatic heating (no time to lose heat). εr EffectiveCore PCB
When to use: finding the effective dielectric constant that a microstrip line "sees" at a given frequency.

Why it matters: Microstrip is a partially open structure — some of the field lives in the dielectric below and some lives in air above. The effective Dk that drives propagation velocity and Z₀ is therefore between 1 (all air) and the bulk Dk (all dielectric). It also rises with frequency above ~1 GHz (Kirschning-Jansen dispersion) as more field gets pulled down into the substrate. Length-tuning a quarter-wave structure without εeff correction can be 5–15 % off at high frequency.

How it works: Hammerstad-Jensen 1980 gives the static (DC) εeff as a function of width, height, and Dk. Kirschning-Jansen 1982 adds the dispersion correction εeff(f) = εr − (εr − εeff_static) / (1 + (f/fT)^a). The result is what other tools (impedance, quarter-wave, etc.) use internally; this tool exposes the raw number.

What you'll enter:

  • Bulk Dk (εr) — substrate dielectric constant at your operating frequency.
  • Trace width (W) and height (h) — same as the impedance tool.
  • Frequency — for the dispersion term.

Without simulating |Z(f)| you can't see whether one of those peaks pokes above target. Sandler's NISM flatness metric (Z_max / Z_min ≤ 2× = 6 dB) is the modern best-practice acceptance criterion.

How it works: Each cap is modelled as a series RLC: Z_cap = ESR + jωL_total + 1/(jωC). Caps in parallel: Y_total = Σ Y_i. Tool plots |Z(f)| log-log across user-specified band, overlays the target-Z line, marks anti-resonance peaks (local maxima where adjacent caps cancel), and computes NISM flatness (worst / best).

What you'll enter:

  • Cap bank — table of (C, ESL + L_mnt, ESR, label) per branch. Add from the MLCC library or type by hand.
  • Sweep band kHz to GHz — typically 10 kHz to 1 GHz.
  • Target impedance mΩ — from the PDN Target tool.
Tip: Putting many caps of the SAME value in parallel is much more robust against anti-resonance than putting one each of many values. Two 22 µF + four 100 nF + six 10 nF is the classic 1990s recipe; modern PI practice often prefers eight 1 µF + eight 100 nF + plane capacitance handling the rest.

RF / Microwave (5 tools)

Pi-Pad AttenuatorRF
When to use: dropping signal level by a fixed dB (test-equipment pads, mixer level-setting, ADC input attenuation, or matching pads between unequal real impedances) using three resistors.

Why it matters: Resistive pads are the simplest, broadest-bandwidth attenuators there are — flat from DC to many GHz if you use low-parasitic 0402 thin-film parts. The "Pi" topology (shunt → series → shunt) is the most common in test equipment because it puts a shunt resistor directly at each port, which dampens out reflections.

How it works: Symmetric (Zin = Zout): R_series = Z₀·(K²−1)/(2K), R_shunt = Z₀·(K+1)/(K−1) where K = 10^(att_dB/20). Asymmetric (Zin ≠ Zout): uses the Chemandy / Lamsim closed form with Vout = √(Zout/Zin)/K accounting for the impedance step. After computing the ideal values, snaps each to E24 standard.

What you'll enter:

  • Source impedance (Zs) Ω — 50, 75, or other.
  • Load impedance (Zl) Ω — same or different.
  • Attenuation dB — typical 3, 6, 10, 20, 30 dB.

T-Pad AttenuatorRF
When to use: same as Pi-pad, but using the dual topology (series → shunt → series). Sometimes fits PCB layout better, sometimes gives gentler resistor values for moderate attenuations.

Why it matters: Same performance as a Pi-pad of equal dB — the choice is layout convenience. T-pads have less-extreme resistor values for moderate 3–10 dB pads; Pi-pads dominate for high attenuation (> 20 dB).

How it works: Symmetric: R_series = Z₀·(K−1)/(K+1) per arm, R_shunt = Z₀·2K/(K²−1). Asymmetric: same Chemandy closed form, dual-topology. E24 snap on each resistor.

L-Match (Impedance)RF
When to use: matching an antenna, mixer, or amplifier port that has some complex impedance (R + jX) to a real Z₀ (usually 50 Ω) at a single frequency.

Why it matters: Two-element matching is the simplest narrowband match. Always solveable, always two valid arrangements (high-pass and low-pass topology), Q is fixed by the impedance ratio. It's the right starting point before reaching for Pi or T which add tuning knobs at the cost of complexity.

How it works: For matching R_L + jX_L to Z₀, there are exactly four valid L-network arrangements (two with series-L/shunt-C, two with shunt-L/series-C, on either side of the load). The tool computes all four, reports the resulting Q (which sets bandwidth: BW ≈ f₀/Q), and tells you which arrangement gives the bandwidth you asked for.

What you'll enter:

  • Source impedance Zs — usually 50 Ω.
  • Load impedance Zl — complex: enter R + jX (e.g. 25 + j40 Ω for an antenna).
  • Centre frequency MHz / GHz.
Tip: If the load is inductive (jX > 0), the C element must be on the load side; if capacitive, the L element goes on the load side. The tool picks automatically.
Quarter-Wave Match (Multi-section)RF
When to use: matching two unequal real impedances (e.g. 50 Ω source to 200 Ω antenna) using a cascade of λ/4 transmission-line sections on the PCB.

Why it matters: A single λ/4 transformer matches across a narrow band (Q ≈ 4 for a 50:200 transform). Multiple sections trade length for bandwidth — three sections roughly triples bandwidth, four quadruples. Binomial gives maximally flat (no in-band ripple) response; Chebyshev gives wider bandwidth at the cost of equal-ripple ringing inside the band.

How it works: Picks per-section characteristic impedances using binomial or Chebyshev synthesis (Pozar §5.5). For each section's Z₀, the inverse-Hammerstad solver finds the microstrip trace width. Each section's electrical λ/4 length is computed using its own εeff (since width affects εeff) — so each section is physically a slightly different length but electrically the same quarter wave.

What you'll enter:

  • Source Z and load Z Ω — must both be real.
  • Number of sections — 1 (narrowest), 2, 3 (common), 4–9 (wide).
  • Design frequency MHz / GHz.
  • Method — Binomial (flat) or Chebyshev (wider but rippled).
  • VSWR bandwidth target — 1.5:1, 2:1, or 3:1.
  • Substrate, height, copper thickness — for the microstrip width solver.
Tip: The per-section table reports both the width and the physical length to use in layout. Physical length differs by a few percent between sections because each section's εeff is different — a wider section has more field in air, lower εeff, slightly longer physical λ/4.
Lumped LC Match (L / Pi / T)RF
When to use: three-element narrow-band matching (Pi or T) when you need more selectivity than the two-element L gives, or when you want to see what real component parasitics do to S11.

Why it matters: Pi and T networks add a Q knob — you trade bandwidth for sharpness. They also let you match between two real impedances at lower or higher Q than the natural L would force. The tool also models the package parasitics of the L/C parts (0402 ESL, ESR, parallel cap) so you can see what S11 the real implementation will hit, not the ideal-component S11.

How it works: Pick topology (L / Pi / T). Specify source Z, load Z (real or complex), centre frequency, and desired loaded Q. Tool solves the network equations and prints the L and C values, then snaps to E12/E24. Each part is then "decorated" with typical package parasitics from the part library, and S11(f) is plotted across a sweep band so you can see the achievable match.

What you'll enter:

  • Topology — L, Pi, or T.
  • Zs, Zl, centre frequency — same as L-match.
  • Loaded Q — typical 2–5 for broad, 10+ for narrow / image-rejection.
  • Component package — 0402, 0603, 0805 — sets the parasitic envelope.

Stackup Symmetry & BowMechanical/DFM

How it works: Tool takes per-layer copper-coverage percentages (manual estimate — 80 % for solid planes, 30 % for typical signal layers, 10 % for sparse) and computes a symmetry score: how close is the centre-of-mass of copper to the geometric centre. What you'll enter:

  • Number of layers.

Paste Aperture (IPC-7525)Mechanical/DFM
When to use: designing the stencil aperture for a tight SMT footprint (0201, 01005, fine-pitch BGA, QFN thermal pad).

Thermal ManagementThermal
When to use: predicting the operating junction temperature of an IC and the margin you have left to Tj_max.

Why it matters: Every 10 °C above rated junction temp roughly halves device life. Thermal Via ArrayThermal
When to use: sizing the via array under a thermally-grounded SMT pad (QFN exposed pad, DC-DC inductor pad, RF PA, MOSFET).

Why it matters: The total θ from an exposed pad to the buried ground plane is dominated by the via array. Doubling the via count drops θ by less than half (diminishing returns from Lee spreading resistance — the heat has to spread laterally to reach all those vias). Plus, solder voiding under the exposed pad can spike the bottleneck by 30 %+ if the design has a poor stencil aperture.

How it works: Three contributions added in series: (1) per-via thermal resistance from the copper-cylinder formula, paralleled across N vias. (2) Lee spreading resistance — heat has to spread laterally from the central die to reach the outer vias; depends on pad-area-to-via-array-area ratio.   • Exposed pad size mm — from the IC datasheet (e.g. 3.1 × 3.1 mm for a QFN 5×5).

  • Via geometry — drill, plating, board thickness.
  • Voiding fraction % — typical 5–10 % for well-designed apertures, 20 %+ for stencil-design issues.

When to use: sizing the inductor and output capacitor for a CCM-mode buck (step-down) or boost (step-up) DC-DC converter.

Why it matters: The inductor sets the inductor ripple current and the boundary between Continuous Conduction Mode and Discontinuous. EMC / EMI (1 tool)

Radiated Emissions — Small LoopEMC
When to use: sanity-checking whether a switching-supply current loop, clock distribution loop, or other small radiating structure will pass FCC Class B / CISPR Class B at 3 m measurement distance — before sending the board to the EMC lab.

Why it matters: Loop area and harmonic content drive radiated E-field. Cutting loop area in half cuts radiation by 6 dB. Knowing this at layout time is 100× cheaper than re-spinning after a failed EMC scan. The classic killers are the input-cap-to-switch-node loop on a buck regulator and the clock-distribution loops on a high-speed digital board.

How it works: Small-loop antenna far-field formula E ≈ (2π·f)² · μ₀ · I · A / (4π·r·c), where I is the current at the harmonic of interest, A is the loop area, r is the measurement distance, and c is c. Tool converts to dBµV/m and compares against FCC and CISPR Class A / B limit lines.

What you'll enter:

  • Loop area mm² — the area of the current loop, projected to the measurement axis. Typical: 10–50 mm² (well-laid-out buck supply), 100–500 mm² (typical), 1000+ mm² (problem).
  • Harmonic current mA — typical: 100 mA fundamental for a 1 A switcher; harmonics drop ~6 dB per octave for square-wave content.
  • Harmonic frequency MHz — n × switching freq.
  • Measurement distance m — 3 m (FCC / CISPR), 10 m (some standards).
Tip: A 10 mm² loop sounds small, but 10 mm² × 100 mA at 100 MHz is already at the FCC Class B limit. Below 100 mm² is achievable with deliberate layout; above 1000 mm² almost guarantees a failure.

When to use: length-tuning the DQ, DQS, ADDR, CMD, and CK signals on a DDR memory bus to JEDEC tolerances.

Why it matters: DDR3 / DDR4 / DDR5 have tight per-byte-group skew tolerances. Typical: DQ within a byte lane ±25 mil to DQS; CK to CMD/ADDR ±10 mil. Get one signal out of spec and the interface fails the training phase at boot — the memory never enumerates.

How it works: Paste in or enter the as-routed lengths of every signal, grouped by byte lane (for DQ/DM) and by command/address (for the rest). Tool computes per-signal deviation from the byte-group reference (the DQS for DQ; the CK for ADDR/CMD), tags signals out of spec, and recommends how many mils to add (via length serpentine) or remove.

What you'll enter:

  • DDR generation — DDR3 / DDR4 / DDR5 / LPDDR4 / LPDDR5 — auto-loads the tolerance table.
  • Per-byte-lane lengths mil / mm — DQ[0–7], DM, DQS+/DQS−, then DQ[8–15] for the next byte, etc.
  • Command/address lengths — ADDR, CMD, CK+/CK−.
Pitfall: "Length match" means electrical length, not physical length.
