Reference (12 tools)

Ohm's LawReference
When to use: any time you know two of voltage, current, resistance, or power, and need the other two.

Why it matters: Ohm's law (V = I·R) and the power equation (P = V·I) are the foundation of every electronics calculation. Getting them right keeps the smoke inside the chips.

In plain English: Tells you how big a resistor to use, how much current a circuit will draw, or how much heat it'll dissipate — given any two of the four numbers.

Conversion CalculatorReference
When to use: converting between units — mils ↔ mm, °C ↔ °F, dB ↔ ratio, rect ↔ polar.

Why it matters: PCB datasheets mix mils, mm, inches, °C, °F, dBm and dBV freely. A single bad conversion can leave a trace 25.4× the right width.

In plain English: Like a Swiss-army knife for any "wait, what's that in mm?" moment. Type the number on one side, read it on the other.

SI Prefix ReferenceReference
When to use: reading a datasheet and trying to remember what zepto, atto, ronto, or quetta mean.

Why it matters: A lookup table that fits on a card. Beats Googling for the 50th time.

In plain English: Every "kilo / mega / nano / pico" prefix and its power-of-ten, in one screen.

Mechanical ReferenceReference
When to use: picking a drill bit, choosing tap size for a standoff thread, or matching a clearance hole to a screw.

Why it matters: Mechanical and electrical PCB tasks usually overlap at the holes — and ANSI / UNC tables aren't memorable.

In plain English: Two searchable tables: every numbered/lettered drill (#80 through Z) with diameters in inch and mm, and every UNC screw size (#4-40, #6-32, 10-32, 1/4-20…) with thread pitch and tap-drill size.

When to use: sizing an off-board wire — power feeders, battery interconnects, motor leads, magnet-wire windings, antenna feeds.

Why it matters: Undersized wire melts insulation or starts fires. Oversized wire wastes weight and cost. Voltage drop on long runs starves the load. This single tool answers all three.

Get the max safe current, voltage drop, wire temperature, fusing current, and warnings for thermal runaway or insulation melt.

Voltage DividerReference
When to use: bringing a higher voltage down to an ADC-friendly range, or building a bias network.

Why it matters: A two-resistor divider is the simplest analog circuit there is, but picking R1 and R2 that survive E-series rounding and don't load the source is fiddly.

In plain English: Enter any two of (Vin, Vout, R1, R2) and it gives you the other two — already rounded to standard E12/E24 part values.

Core PCB (13 tools)

Conductor Impedance (analytic)Core PCB
When to use: dialing in trace width on a controlled-impedance line — 50 Ω single-ended, 100 Ω diff, or a custom value.

Why it matters: If a trace isn't the right Z₀, you get reflections, ringing, EMI, and lost margin on every fast edge. The analytic formulas (Hammerstad-Jensen microstrip, Wadell stripline, Ghione-Naldi coplanar) are what every fab house, board-house Z-coupon calculator, and field solver uses for first-pass design.

In plain English: Pick the topology (microstrip / stripline / coplanar with ground), enter dielectric height, copper thickness, εr — and either solve for width given Z₀, or read Z₀ given a width.

Trace Impedance (openEMS 3D)Core PCB
When to use: when the analytic formula's assumptions break — embedded microstrip, asymmetric stripline, weird coplanar geometries, or anywhere you want a second-opinion sanity check.

Why it matters: Analytic equations are calibrated for "ideal" geometries; real boards have soldermask, etch slope, asymmetric reference planes, and edge-of-board effects that shift Z₀ by 2–5%. A 3D field solver handles all of that. The closed-form analytic answer is shown alongside so you can see the discrepancy.

In plain English: Wraps openEMS (a free 3D electromagnetic simulator) inside the GUI. Pick a topology, set the geometry, hit "Run 3D Simulation" — wait 30 s to a few minutes, get a measured Z₀ from a real FDTD field simulation. Also has a "Preview 3D Geometry" button so you can eyeball the structure before running.

Fusing Current (Onderdonk)Core PCB
When to use: finding the fault-current that would melt a PCB trace, or designing a deliberate sacrificial trace as a fuse.

Why it matters: A trace can survive 10× its steady-state ampacity for a few milliseconds. Knowing the actual melt-current helps you specify upstream protection (or refuse to specify upstream protection if the trace itself is the fuse).

In plain English: Enter trace cross-section (or just AWG / width × thickness), ambient, and the duration the fault lasts. Get the current at which the trace reaches copper's melting point (1085 °C).

εr EffectiveCore PCB
When to use: finding the effective dielectric constant a microstrip line "sees" at a given frequency.

Why it matters: Microstrip lives partly in air and partly in substrate, so its εeff is somewhere between 1 and the bulk Dk. Above ~1 GHz, εeff also rises with frequency (Kirschning-Jansen dispersion). Length-tuning RF circuits without this correction puts a 5–15% error on every quarter-wave line.

In plain English: Enter Dk, width, height, frequency → get εeff with proper dispersion correction. Mostly used internally by other tools, but exposed here when you want the raw number.

Bandwidth & Max LengthCore PCB
When to use: deciding whether a trace needs end-termination, and how long it can be before reflections matter.

Why it matters: Below the bandwidth knee (≈ 0.5 / rise_time), a trace is "short" and lumped. Above it the trace is a transmission line and you must terminate. Most digital design problems live exactly at this boundary.

In plain English: Enter rise time (or clock frequency), εeff, get the knee frequency and the maximum trace length (about λ/6 of the knee) before termination becomes mandatory.

Via PropertiesCore PCB
When to use: any time you're putting a via in a current-carrying or RF path.

Why it matters: A via has parasitic R, L, C, a self Z₀, and a current-carrying limit — all of which matter for high-speed signal vias, decoupling-cap mounting, and high-current power vias. Howard Johnson's standard via-inductance formula (L = 5.08·h·ln(4h/d)) is the universal reference.

In plain English: Enter drill, plating, board thickness — get DC resistance, parasitic inductance (in three common conventions: no-constant / +0.5 / +1.0), capacitance to the surrounding antipad, characteristic impedance of the barrel, and a per-via thermal resistance. Also computes the equivalent for an array of N vias in parallel.

Differential Via StubCore PCB
When to use: deciding the back-drill depth (or whether to back-drill at all) for a differential through-via on a high-speed pair.

Why it matters: The unused bottom of a through-via is a quarter-wave open stub. Once stub_length ≈ λ/4 at your Nyquist frequency, the channel gets a sharp ~20 dB notch right in your eye. Most PCIe-Gen5+, 100GbE, and OIF-CEI links can't ship without back-drilling.

In plain English: Tell it your protocol (PCIe, USB-C/SS, 100GbE…) and the via depth — get the maximum stub it tolerates before the eye closes. See also Via Stub Impact for the full S21 / eye / budget version.

Planar Spiral InductorCore PCB
When to use: designing an on-PCB spiral coil — RFID, antenna match, low-pass output filter inductor, wireless-charging coil.

Why it matters: Discrete chip inductors max out around 470 nH at sane current; for bigger values or where you want a custom Q, you wind it into the copper. The Modified-Wheeler formula (Mohan et al. 1999) is the most-cited closed-form predictor.

In plain English: Enter number of turns, inner diameter, conductor width, spacing. Get the inductance, DC resistance, and self-resonant frequency.

Embedded ResistorCore PCB
When to use: specifying buried thin-film resistors (Ohmega-Ply, TICER, NiCr) on a high-density board.

Why it matters: Removing thousands of discrete 0402 resistors by burying them in a planar layer saves real estate and lowers parasitic inductance — but you have to size the L/W ratio against the sheet resistance.

In plain English: R = R_sheet · (length / width). Enter the sheet resistance the foil vendor quotes (50 Ω/sq, 100 Ω/sq, 250 Ω/sq, 1000 Ω/sq are typical) plus your length and width, get the resistance. Or solve in reverse.

Dielectric Dk/Df vs FrequencySignal Integrity
When to use: when your laminate datasheet only gives Dk/Df at one frequency (typically 10 GHz) and you need the value at another frequency for an SI calculation.

Why it matters: Dk drops with frequency (typ. 4.5 at 1 MHz to 3.9 at 20 GHz for FR-4). Df rises with frequency. Using the wrong number throws off impedance, propagation delay, and insertion loss budgets at the few-percent level — which matters for tight-tolerance designs.

In plain English: Pick a laminate (Megtron 6, Rogers RO4350B, FR-4, etc.) from the dropdown — it loads the datasheet point and fits a causal Djordjevic-Sarkar model. Enter the target frequency, get Dk/Df at that frequency. Or pick "Custom" and enter your own datasheet numbers.

Conductor Loss (Roughness)Signal Integrity
When to use: estimating how much extra loss the copper roughness on your foil adds vs. an ideal smooth conductor.

Why it matters: Above ~5 GHz, foil tooth-height contributes more loss than the underlying skin-effect math predicts. Without correction, your channel insertion-loss budget under-estimates real loss by 1.5–3×.

In plain English: Pick the foil class (STD / RTF / VLP / HVLP / Hyper-VLP) and frequency. Get a multiplier (e.g. K = 2.1 at 10 GHz on a 4 μm Rz foil) that you apply on top of the smooth-conductor loss. Uses Cannonball-Huray as the default model (the industry standard, verified against Polar Si9000e).

Channel Insertion Loss BudgetSignal Integrity
When to use: tallying up the total dB loss of a high-speed channel from end to end.

Why it matters: Every SerDes spec has a hard IL ceiling (e.g. PCIe Gen5 = 36 dB total at Nyquist). Hit that ceiling and the link won't train. Knowing where your dB is going — dielectric vs conductor vs roughness vs stub vs connector — tells you what to optimize first.

In plain English: Enter trace length, geometry, laminate, foil class, Nyquist frequency, and connector loss. Get a per-component breakdown of the dB loss budget across the channel.

Jitter Budget (Tj)Signal Integrity
When to use: verifying a serial link will meet its bit-error-rate target given component jitter specs.

Why it matters: SerDes specs allocate jitter across the TX, channel, and RX. The dual-Dirac model lets you predict total peak-to-peak jitter at an arbitrary BER (1e-12, 1e-15) from the per-source random and deterministic components.

In plain English: Enter random jitter (RJ, σ in ps) and deterministic jitter (DJ, p-p in ps) for each source. Get the total Tj at your target BER plotted on an eye-diagram backdrop.

Power Integrity (4 tools)

PDN Target & Plane CapPower Integrity
When to use: computing the target impedance your power-distribution network has to hit, and what the bare GND/PWR plane pair contributes for free.

Why it matters: Z_target = ΔV_allowed / ΔI_transient is the foundational PDN-design equation (Smith & Bogatin 2017). Plane capacitance (ε·A/h) handles the high-frequency end "for free" if the plane pair is tight.

In plain English: Enter rail voltage, allowed ripple % (1% typical for digital, 50 mV for FPGA core), and peak transient current. Get the impedance ceiling. Also enter plane area and Dk to see the natural plane capacitance and the Xc-frequency knee where the planes alone stop being enough.

Why it matters: A "10 µF 10 V X7R 0603" datasheet'd at 0 V often delivers 4 µF at 5 V bias. Your PDN model is wrong by that much if you use the sticker value.

In plain English: Pick the part from the library (or enter the curve manually). Enter your actual bias voltage and operating temperature. Get the real effective capacitance, both at room temp and over the temperature range.

When to use: figuring the parasitic loop inductance from a decoupling cap's pad, through the via, down to the plane, and back up.

Cap Bank Analyzer (Z(f))Power Integrity
When to use: verifying a parallel decoupling-cap network meets the target impedance across the full band, before you commit to a BoM.

Why it matters: The classic "spread cap values logarithmically across the band" approach creates anti-resonance peaks between every adjacent SRF pair. Without simulating the |Z(f)| curve you can't see whether one of those peaks pokes above the target. Sandler's NISM flatness metric (Z_max/Z_min ≤ 2 = 6 dB) is the modern best-practice acceptance criterion.

In plain English: Build a parallel cap bank from the MLCC library or by typing in C / ESL / ESR per branch. Plot |Z(f)| vs the target line, see the anti-resonance peaks and the NISM flatness number.

RF / Microwave (5 tools)

Pi-Pad AttenuatorRF
When to use: dropping signal level by a fixed dB (e.g. test-equipment pads, mixer level-setting, ADC input attenuation) using three resistors.

Why it matters: Resistive pads are the simplest, broadest-band attenuators there are — flat from DC to many GHz if you pick low-parasitic 0402s. The "Pi" topology (shunt-series-shunt) is the most common.

In plain English: Enter source Z, load Z, and dB of attenuation. Get the three resistor values, snapped to E24 standard parts. Symmetric (same Z both sides) and asymmetric (Zin ≠ Zout, "matching pad") are both supported.

T-Pad AttenuatorRF
When to use: same as Pi-pad, but for the dual topology (series-shunt-series). Sometimes lays out cleaner.

Why it matters: Topologically identical performance to a Pi-pad of equal dB. Pick whichever fits the layout better. T-pads tend to have less-extreme resistor values for moderate attenuations (3–10 dB).

In plain English: Same workflow as Pi-pad — three resistors, E24-snapped.

L-Match (Impedance)RF
When to use: matching an antenna, mixer, or amplifier port that has some complex impedance (R+jX) to a real Z₀ (usually 50 Ω) at a single frequency.

Why it matters: Two-element matching is the simplest narrowband match there is, and it's the right starting point before you go to Pi or T. Always-correct math, narrowband Q.

In plain English: Enter the complex load impedance, source impedance, and target frequency. Get the L and C values for the four possible L-network arrangements, plus the resulting Q (which sets bandwidth).

Quarter-Wave Match (Multi-section)RF
When to use: matching two unequal real impedances (e.g. 50 Ω to 200 Ω) with a multi-section transmission-line transformer.

Why it matters: More sections = wider bandwidth in exchange for length. Binomial (maximally flat) is monotonic; Chebyshev (equal ripple) is broader-band. Both classics from Pozar.

In plain English: Enter source Z, load Z, number of sections, and design frequency. Get per-section impedance, per-section trace width (from Hammerstad-Jensen inverse), and per-section physical length (each section uses its own εeff so each is electrically a true λ/4). Outputs a VSWR-vs-frequency plot for verification.

Lumped LC Match (L / Pi / T)RF
When to use: three-element narrow-band matching (Pi or T) when you need more selectivity than the two-element L can give, or when you're stuck with specific component package parasitic.

Why it matters: Pi/T networks add a Q knob — you trade bandwidth for sharpness. The tool also models the package parasitics of the L/C parts so you can see what S11 the real implementation will hit, not the ideal-component answer.

In plain English: Choose L / Pi / T, set the source and load impedances, the centre frequency, and your desired Q. Get the component values snapped to E-series, with a frequency-sweep S11 plot showing what a 0402 part's parasitic L/C does to the result.

Mechanical & DFM (4 tools)

Padstack CalculatorMechanical/DFM
When to use: sizing pads, lands, and clearances for thru-holes, BGAs, and discrete SMDs.

Why it matters: A pad that's too small loses copper after etch; too big and you can't route between BGA balls. Every fab house has minimum annular-ring rules, and a real layout has to plan to those rules.

In plain English: Four sub-tools: thru-hole pad sizing (lead + clearance + annular ring), BGA land sizing, pad-clearance rule, and corner-to-corner pad pitch.

Stackup Symmetry & BowMechanical/DFM
When to use: evaluating whether a stackup design will warp during reflow.

Why it matters: Asymmetric copper density top-vs-bottom is the #1 cause of board warpage. Warped boards fail BGA reflow, fail mechanical fit-up, and fail in-circuit test. IPC-6012E sets the bow/twist limits.

In plain English: Enter per-layer copper coverage (rough estimate: 80% for power planes, 30% for signal layers). Get a symmetry score and a board-size-dependent IPC-6012E pass/fail.

Paste Aperture (IPC-7525)Mechanical/DFM
When to use: designing the stencil aperture for a tight SMT footprint (0201, 01005, fine-pitch BGA, QFN thermal pad).

Why it matters: If the aperture's area-ratio (opening area / wall area) drops below ≈ 0.66, paste won't release cleanly and you get skips. IPC-7525B is the universal standard. For most paste types you also want aspect ratio (width/thickness) ≥ 1.5.

In plain English: Enter aperture width, length, and stencil thickness. Get both ratios with a pass/fail.

BGA Escape RoutingMechanical/DFM
When to use: planning the layer count needed to fully break out a BGA at a given pitch.

Why it matters: BGA pin count grows faster than perimeter, so beyond ~25 pins you can't escape all signals from a single layer. Figuring out the layer count before you commit to a stackup avoids expensive re-spins.
