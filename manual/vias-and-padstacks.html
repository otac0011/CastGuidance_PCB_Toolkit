Vias and padstacks

Holes and the copper around them: electrical behaviour, reliability limits, and land geometry that the fab can actually build.

Via Properties

Everything electrical and thermal about a plated through-hole.

Via type dropdown. Two-layer or multi-layer. Microvias have their own tool — see Microvia Analyzer.

Inputs. Finished hole diameter, plating thickness (1 mil / 25 µm is typical), capture pad diameter D1, antipad diameter D2, board thickness, substrate εr, allowed ΔT for the ampacity calculation, the applied DC current, and a via count for parallel arrays.

Outputs. Barrel cross-section, DC resistance, voltage drop, inductance (Howard Johnson form), capacitance, barrel Z₀, resonant frequency, the step rise time the via can pass, aspect ratio, and thermal resistance θ for one via and for the array.

Insight. Via inductance is dominated by length, not diameter — doubling the drill diameter buys you very little, while halving the barrel length halves the inductance. This is why the fix for an inductive via is a shorter stack, not a bigger hole. Aspect ratio (board thickness ÷ drill diameter) above about 10:1 starts to strain plating throwing power; above 14:1 most fabs will quote rather than list a price.

The via network

Under the closed-form outputs, a card runs EyeOpener Lite's via models on the same drill, pad, antipad, board thickness and εr. It answers what the closed forms cannot:

  • what the via looks like to a signal crossing it;
  • how much it loses;
  • whether its stub resonates in your band.

Its own inputs are the copper layer count, the exit layer, Df, the trace impedance, the trace widths, the number of ground vias round the signal via and their distance, and an optional back-drill. Every other copper layer is taken as a GND plane, and only the entry and exit layers have pads (non-functional pads removed).

It reports:

  • the via's L and C;
  • its impedance √(L/C) and delay √(LC);
  • |S21| and |S11| at your frequency;
  • an |S21| plot to 40 GHz;
  • for a stub, its solved notch, the frequency it may resonate from, and where the network brackets the notch;
  • S-param quality: the swept 2-port through the passivity, reciprocity, sampling and causality screen. A failing check is listed as a message.

Two cores: full wave and network

  • Full wave (the default on a desktop). The barrel, pads, antipads, planes and stub are one field, solved round the via's axis (an axisymmetric finite element solve). It catches what the parts miss: neighbouring plane pairs coupling through the antipads, and the stub's own wave.
  • The network. The barrel's static capacitance to every plane, each plane pair as a cavity with the evanescent modes the barrel excites (Li et al. 2023). About a hundred times faster. Its notch with and without those modes is the bracket shown beside the full wave.

Both close each plane pair with the ground vias that tie it.

The legs and the trace widths

Over its plane's antipad a trace has no plane under it, and it then crosses the pad to the barrel. Include the legs adds both, each solved by the 2-D field solver. Without them a via lacks about 100 pH.

The trace widths default to the ones that give the trace impedance on their layers: a microstrip on the top dielectric for the entry, a stripline or microstrip for the exit. Untick the box to enter your own; the card shows each trace's impedance.

Speed

A new geometry takes a few seconds on a desktop, then it is cached: changing the frequency of interest is instant. A progress line shows each stage.

In the browser the card solves the network without the legs, once and then on Solve. Solve full wave runs the full wave with the legs; allow a minute or more there.

How far to trust the via models

  • Against openEMS, on a six-layer via with its traces: the full wave puts the stub notch at 16.85 GHz against 17.35 (−2.9 %), with |S| within 0.05 to 8 GHz, with and without ground vias.
  • Against a 3-D FEM, five six-layer stubs: the full wave reads the notch 1–7 % low; the network 8–17 % high, and without its evanescent modes 0.6–10 % low.
  • Against HFSS, on Li et al.'s published via through a plate pair (7, 10 and 15 mil antipads): the network within 0.15 dB to 40 GHz.

Stitching, dense stacks and the limits

  • Stitching matters. With no ground via near the signal via, a via whose return changes plane pours power into the unstitched pair. The card warns and shows the loss. Add a ground via within about 1 mm.
  • Many closely spaced planes can load a stub more than the quick bound (quarter wave ÷ 1.75) allows. When the solved notch falls below the bound, the card says so: trust the solved notch.
  • Above 40 GHz the results are shown but not validated. Plating, pad size and layer shift move a real via more than the model's error does.

The models are EyeOpener Lite's, ported with their tests; see Verification and limits.

Microvia Analyzer (HDI)

A dedicated analyzer for laser-drilled microvias, with return-path-aware inductance and the HDI reliability advisories that through-hole tools miss.

Microvia geometry. Drill diameter at the top of the pocket (75–100 µm typical), depth (one dielectric layer, 50–100 µm), side-wall plating thickness (8–15 µm), bottom-pocket plating (leave at 0 for auto — 70 % of the side-wall, a typical conical-pocket ratio), capture pad and antipad diameters, and the return-via spacing s, centre-to-centre to the nearest stitching via.

Return-via spacing drives the inductance more than any other input. A microvia's loop inductance is set by the area enclosed between the signal via and wherever the return current gets back — put a stitching via 0.5 mm away and the loop is tiny; leave it 5 mm away and the microvia's advantage evaporates.

Topology. Connection target (Plane, or Trace — landing on a signal stub raises an inductance-uncertainty warning), stack height, and stack topology: Single, Staggered (preferred above 2 levels) or Stacked (risky above 2).

Operating conditions. Substrate εr, ambient, applied current, ΔT for ampacity, frequency for skin effect, and signal rise time for the lumped-versus-distributed regime check.

Outputs. Grouped into Electrical (inductance via the return-path-aware Howard Johnson form, capacitance via the Gunston elliptic-coaxial formula), Regime (is this still a lumped element at your edge rate), and Thermal / Ampacity. Beside the inductance, Goldfarb–Pucel's via-to-plane inductance is shown for a microvia landing on a solid plane: a 3-D field solver confirmed it within 2 % for depths of 100–631 µm. Reliability advisories — aspect-ratio limits, weak-microvia-interface and first-reflow warnings per IPC-WP-023 — appear in the diagnostics strip.

Insight. The weak microvia interface (WMI) is the industry's main microvia failure mode: an incomplete metallurgical bond at the target pad that survives electrical test and then opens during thermal cycling or even during the first reflow. Stacked microvias concentrate stress at exactly that interface, which is why staggering is preferred once you go beyond two levels.

Padstack Calculator

Four small sub-tools sharing one page, selected from a dropdown.

Thru-hole pad. Inputs: hole diameter, minimum external annular ring, minimum internal annular ring, thermal isolation gap. Outputs: external and internal pad diameters, antipad, thermal-relief inner diameter and spoke width, per IPC-2221 minimums.

BGA land. The quick rule of thumb: NSMD land = 0.8 × ball, SMD land = ball, with a fabrication tolerance applied. For real BGA work use the BGA Pad Designer — this sub-tool has no pitch awareness, no mask-opening output and no registration input.

Conductor clearance (1 or 2 conductors). Given a pitch and your trace width and spacing, returns the largest pad diameter that still lets one or two traces pass between adjacent holes, and the annular ring that leaves.

Corner-to-corner. The Pythagorean diagonal of a rectangular pad or lead, plus a suggested drill window at 1.05–1.15× that diagonal.

BGA Pad Designer (NSMD / SMD)

Sizes the complete BGA land — copper, solder mask and stencil aperture — for both mask constructions at once, and answers the specific question "what is the smallest pad that still meets my fabricator's solder-mask registration?".

Every input and every output on this page is in whichever unit you choose in the Units dropdown at the top, so you can work entirely in mm or entirely in mils.

Choosing the geometry

The Ball geometry preset dropdown is a shortcut, not a parts library. It lists the standard pitch and nominal-ball combinations a BGA comes in — 0.35 mm pitch through 1.27 mm — each tagged with the IPC-7351B density level that ball diameter falls into. "Custom" leaves whatever you already typed alone.

Whatever your package drawing's "b" typical dimension actually says, type that in. Use the typical value, not the min or the max: the IPC land size is derived from the nominal ball.

Ball pitch is the finest pitch on the part if it has more than one region. Pitch does not change the IPC target land — it sets the ceilings, i.e. how much copper and how much mask dam physically fit.

Package-side pad Ø is optional. If the vendor publishes the pad or mask opening on the package itself, enter it and the tool compares it against the board-side target. Leave it at 0 to skip the check.

Fabricator capability

Mask registration ± per side is the single most important input. It is how far your fab's solder mask can shift relative to the copper. 1 mil is the default and a reasonable modern assumption; use 2 mil for a low-cost fab, and 25 µm or better if your fab images the mask with LDI. Every headline answer on the page moves when you change it.

The rest describe your fab's limits: land design tolerance (IPC-7351B's ±0.05 mm design window), minimum solder-mask dam (3 mil is the usual floor), minimum copper-to-copper gap, finished copper thickness (only affects the NSMD wetted-area figure), and stencil thickness for the paste-release check.

The headline answers

  • Smallest SMD copper pad = mask opening + 2 × registration. Below this the mask can slip off the copper edge, and you get a partially mask-defined joint whose diameter nobody controls.
  • Smallest NSMD mask opening = land + 2 × registration. The mirror image: below this the mask can creep onto the land and shrink the joint.

NSMD versus SMD

NSMD — the copper land is smaller than the mask opening. Solder anchors the land's top face and its etched sidewall, which is why IPC-7095 makes it the default recommendation. The typical land is the IPC-7351B reduction from the ball diameter; min and max are that ± the land tolerance, with max clamped down if the pitch will not allow it.

SMD — the copper is larger than the mask opening, so the mask sets the joint diameter and must land on copper everywhere. The minimum copper pad is the registration-driven answer; the typical column follows the industry convention of drawing the copper at the ball diameter.

Both cards also report the mask dam and copper gap at the typical size (compare these against your fab's minimums), the wetted solder area, and an estimated collapsed standoff height.

Where the land size comes from

IPC-7351B sizes a BGA land as a percentage reduction from the nominal ball diameter, keyed on the ball diameter alone:

Density level Nominal ball Reduction Multiplier
C (minimum) 0.15 – 0.20 mm 15 % × 0.85
B (nominal) 0.25 – 0.50 mm 20 % × 0.80
A (maximum) 0.55 – 0.75 mm 25 % × 0.75

A ball on a boundary, or in one of the gaps the published table leaves, takes the lower band's percentage. IPC then applies a ±0.05 mm design tolerance, which is where the min and max columns come from.

Supporting outputs

Wetted area quantifies the NSMD-versus-SMD trade instead of asserting it: NSMD adds the land's copper sidewall to the joint, SMD does not.

Collapsed standoff (est.) comes from a constant-volume truncated-sphere model — the ball's solder volume is conserved and the joint becomes a sphere truncated by the two wetting planes. It is a geometry estimate, not a reflow simulation: it ignores surface tension, package weight and printed paste volume. Use it comparatively, to see how standoff falls as the land grows.

Max stencil foil is the thickest stencil that still clears the IPC-7525B 0.66 area-ratio floor for this aperture. Fine-pitch apertures routinely fail on a general-purpose 4 mil foil, and a thickness is a more useful answer than a verdict.

Insight. At 0.5 mm pitch and below, the NSMD-versus-SMD decision is usually made by your fab's mask registration, not by reliability theory. Enter the number they actually quote you, check whether the SMD minimum still leaves a workable copper gap, and choose from that.
