Verification and limits

How the numbers are checked

The validation suite

Every calculator is checked against a reference it shares no code with: an exact solution, a published solver or measurement, a vendor datasheet, or a standard's own equation. Each check has a tolerance fixed before the comparison. The results are the validation scoreboard: one chip per tool (PASS, FAIL or GAP), and for every benchmark its reference, tolerance, worst error, every row and, where curves are compared, the curves.

The suite runs with the toolkit's tests. Each benchmark records a fingerprint of the code it came from; when that code changes, the tests fail until the benchmark is recorded again, so the scoreboard always shows what the shipped code computes.

What PASS, FAIL and GAP mean

  • PASS — every benchmark that checks the tool is inside its tolerance. partial beside it means a part of the tool is still unchecked.
  • FAIL — a benchmark found the tool outside its reference. Every failure is a known one: the math is kept as it was for parity with the original toolkit, the tool now warns where the number is shown, and the miss is pinned so it cannot drift silently.
  • GAP — nothing checks the tool yet. The scoreboard and the source's gap list say what it would take.

The scoreboard in numbers

On 2026-10-26: 64 benchmarks, 555 gated checks, 535 within tolerance and 20 known failures. Of the 51 tools, 36 pass (13 with a part still unchecked), 3 have a known failure (Trace Impedance, Differential Pair Designer, Radiated Emissions) and 12 have no external check yet.

Kinds of reference

From strongest: exact (an analytic answer or a definition), measured hardware, published tools (commercial solvers' results in a paper), datasheet values, an independent tool run on the same input (SciPy, an independently written solver), the validated solver (the toolkit's own 2D solver, itself checked against exact answers), a closed form with a stated accuracy, and self-consistency (a model's own limits).

The parity harness and the unit tests

The toolkit began as a Python application. The .NET rewrite is checked against committed fixtures generated from that original — over fifteen thousand parity assertions covering every calculator's numeric output. Parity proves the port, not the physics: that is what the validation suite is for. Unit tests cover what neither sees: boundary conditions, diagnostics, unit round-trips.

Every formula names its source. Each calculator carries citations to the standard or paper it implements, so the answer to "where does this number come from" is in the tool.

2D solver method, speed and limits

The numerical method — box-integration finite volumes on a graded rectilinear grid, solved twice (cells growing 1.35× and 1.15× from a 160th of the smallest feature) and Richardson-extrapolated — is the one EyeOpener Lite validates the same way. A solve takes 25–550 ms on a desktop (about 110 ms for a single microstrip, 350 ms for a microstrip pair). What it does not model: frequency dispersion (it is quasi-static) and anything that is not a uniform cross-section. Copper loss is extracted only where a loss tool asks for it (see below); the Trace Impedance tool does not show it.

2D conductor loss against Wheeler and Pozar

The Insertion Loss Budget and Conductor Loss tools take R(f) from the same solver: the surface charge on every conductor (unit charge on each line in turn), squared and integrated round the strip and its return, times the surface resistance, blended with DC as √(Rdc² + Rac²). Wheeler's rule (R = (Rs/μ₀)·∂L/∂n, the inductance's change as every copper face recedes) reaches R without the surface charge at all, so it is an independent check. At DC the resistance is exactly 1/(σ·w·t).

Roughness and dielectrics

The roughness factor (Cannonball-Huray, Hammerstad-Jensen) only scales the trace's and the planes' shares; it is checked in its limits (1 when the skin depth dwarfs the teeth, its ceiling far above), not against measured boards — treat rough-copper numbers as the model's, not as validated.

The causal dielectric every Dk/Df is moved with is checked to be what it claims: summing its continuum of Debye poles numerically gives the closed form to 10⁻⁶, so Dk(f) and Df(f) are causal by construction, and the fit returns the datasheet point exactly. The laminate builder reproduces Panasonic's Megtron 6 and 7 Dk to 0.03 (EyeOpener Lite's laminate research has the per-family medians: 0.00–0.07 in Dk and 0.4–7 µm in thickness).

Clemens Method limits

The tables cover W/H 0.1–8, T/H 0.01–0.8, εr 2–11 and each topology's gaps and heights over a stated range. Outside them, and with solder mask, the tools fall back to a real solve or the closed form and say so. A table is fingerprinted to the solver's source, and a test fails if the solver changes and the tables are not regenerated.

Flex: lines over a cross-hatched plane

The flex option combines two solves:

  • the 2D solver's solid-plane answer;
  • the ratios of L and C over the hatch to a solid plane, from a 3D electrostatic and a 3D magnetostatic solve of one period.

It was checked against openEMS FDTD of a real flex board: the Okayama FPC (C. Wang, IEICE Electronics Express 2020). It has 70 µm lines 100 µm apart on 25 µm polyimide, and 105 µm bars at a 500 µm pitch. Four line positions were run, at 0° and at 45°.

Flex hatch against a measured series

Fujikura's US 9,795,027 B2 lists, from TDR on 100 mm lines, the width that gave 50 Ω over an inclined grid at seven copper shares. With only the Dk set from the solid-plane row (80 µm), the tool reads the other six widths at a mean of 50.1–52.1 Ω, as the width grows from 95 to 350 µm.

The patent's speed column disagrees: it says the line gets up to 40 % faster, and the tool says about 10 % slower. FDTD of the 44 % row agrees with the tool (V/V₀ ≈ 0.90; the patent says 1.158), as do NPL's measured delays and Chien 1994. The speed column is shown but not gated.

No board yet has everything. A patent is not peer reviewed, and neither source gives a measured solid line plus a microsection. The tool's impedance is well supported; absolute accuracy on a board you have not calibrated is not proven.

Limits.

  • The solve is low-frequency (quasi-static). Above the frequency where the hatch cell is about a tenth of a wavelength, expect dispersion and loss it does not model.
  • The hatch must repeat along the line.
  • Only microstrip lines over one hatched plane are covered.

DFM, thermal and reference tools

Stencil area and aspect ratios, thermal via conduction, Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws, the SI prefixes (BIPM), screw-thread diameters (ASME B1.1) and the unit conversions all match their definitions exactly. The Thermal Via Array's spreading term is exact for copper much thicker than the pad, which a PCB plane is not; read it as a lower bound.

What "engineering-grade" means

Several tools carry that phrase. It means the answer is good enough to make a design decision with, and not good enough to be a specification or a contract.

Concretely:

  • The PCB Cost Estimator is typically within ±25 % of a real quote. Use it to compare options; get a real quote before you buy.
  • The Radiated Emissions tool is a first-order screen. It will tell you which harmonic is your problem and whether one layout is better than another. It will not tell you that you will pass the chamber.
  • The collapsed standoff in the BGA Pad Designer is a geometry estimate, not a reflow simulation.
  • The analytic impedance formulas are fits with validated ranges. Outside those ranges they degrade quietly, which is why the tool shows each one's distance from the 2D field solver — that distance is your warning.
  • The 2D field solver is quasi-static and lossless in its impedances: a uniform line's Z₀ and εeff, not a launch or a discontinuity.

Where a number is an estimate, the tool says so in the output label or the help panel. Nothing is presented as more certain than it is.

What the toolkit does not do

An honest list, so you do not go looking for something that is not there.

It does not read your design. No Gerber, ODB++, IPC-2581, netlist or CAD project import. No stackup file import. No IBIS or IBIS-AMI. No Touchstone import — the LC ladder writes .s2p files but cannot load one. See Files and exports.

It does not do full-board simulation. The 2D field solver and the bundled openEMS solver extract characteristic impedance for a transmission-line cross-section. They do not simulate a board, a package, a connector, or a channel end to end.

It does not do time-domain or statistical channel simulation. There is no eye diagram from an impulse response, no bathtub curve from a real channel, no IBIS-AMI link training. The eye-closure numbers in the Via Stub tool are analytic estimates from the stub's frequency response, not a simulated eye.

It does not do thermal or mechanical FEA. The thermal tools are lumped-resistance networks.

It does not check DRC or verify a layout. It tells you what the rules should be; it cannot tell you whether your board follows them.

It does not compare two revisions of anything, and it has no project or document model — inputs reset when you reopen a tool.

It does not quote. The cost estimator models published price structures; it is not connected to any fab and cannot place an order.

It does not phone home. No account, no telemetry, no upload of your inputs.

When to distrust a result

  • When the analytic impedance estimates disagree with the 2D field solver. You are outside their fitted range; use the 2D answer. IPC-2141 and the Differential Pair Designer's closed form read off on ordinary boards (see above). Run the 3D solver only for effects a cross-section cannot show.
  • When a diagnostic says the inputs are outside a standard's validated range, or a validation warning names the number you are reading.
  • When you have given a tool bulk Dk where it asked for ε_eff, or the other way round. This is the most common input error in the whole toolkit and it produces plausible-looking wrong answers. See Units and conventions.
  • When the answer depends on a datasheet number quoted at a different frequency than your design. Move it first with the Dielectric Dk/Df tool.
  • When a manufacturability number matters and you have not asked your fab. The defaults in the DFM tools are industry-typical, not your fab's actual capability. Their quoted numbers always win.

Reporting a wrong number

If a result looks wrong, the useful report contains: the tool, every input value, the number you got, the number you expected, and where your expected number came from. The citations in the tool tell you which standard the toolkit implemented — half of apparent disagreements turn out to be two different standards, both correctly implemented.
