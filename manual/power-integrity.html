Power integrity and power electronics

Keeping a rail quiet: what impedance you need, what your capacitors actually deliver, and what the mounting costs you.

PDN Target and Plane Cap

Sets the target impedance for a power rail and works out how much of it the plane capacitance alone can cover.

Inputs. Voltage rail (0.85 V, 1.0 V core, 1.8 V, 3.3 V, 5 V…), allowed ripple as a percentage or in mV, maximum current, and the transient fraction — what proportion of the maximum current is the actual step. Then the plane geometry: width, length, separation, dielectric εr, and the frequency of interest.

Outputs. Z_target from V_ripple ÷ I_transient, plane capacitance from the parallel-plate formula, the plane's Xc at your frequency, and the Xc-to-Z_target ratio — which tells you the frequency above which the planes alone stop being enough and discrete decoupling has to take over.

The core idea. A power delivery network is a filter problem, not a capacitance problem. What the load cares about is the impedance the rail presents at every frequency in the transient's spectrum: Z_target = ΔV_allowed / ΔI_transient. A 1.0 V rail allowing 3 % ripple with a 10 A step needs 30 mV / 10 A = 3 mΩ, flat, out to the edge rate.

Insight. The transient fraction input matters more than people expect. The step is what the PDN sees, not the total current — a 20 A rail with a 20 % transient is a 4 A step and a much easier target than a 20 A step would be.

MLCC DC-Bias Derating

What a ceramic capacitor actually delivers once it is biased and warm.

Inputs. Nominal capacitance (the sticker value), rated voltage, the voltage you will actually apply, and the operating temperature.

Outputs. Effective capacitance under bias, the applied-to-rated voltage ratio, and a combined retention percentage broken into its voltage and temperature components.

Why this exists. Class II ceramics (X5R, X7R, Y5V) lose capacitance under DC bias, and the loss is severe. A part marked "10 µF 10 V X7R 0603" is measured at 0 V; at 5 V bias it commonly delivers 4 µF. Design a PDN with sticker values and you will be short by a factor of two.

Insight. Headroom and package size are what protect you. A 10 V part at 10 % bias keeps around 95 %; a 6.3 V part at 80 % bias often drops to 20–30 %. A larger case size of the same value and voltage always derates less, because the dielectric is thicker. C0G/NP0 does not derate with bias at all, but is only available at small values.

Mounting Inductance (L_mnt)

The parasitic loop inductance a decoupling capacitor picks up from its pads and vias — usually larger than the capacitor's own ESL.

Inputs. Via height from the capacitor pad down to the reference plane, via diameter (8–12 mil typical), pad length, and the pad-to-via separation; for the AN 958 route also the power-to-GND via spacing and the capacitor's case size.

Outputs. Total mounting inductance, split into the via and pad/trace contributions, plus single-via inductance for reference.

The AN 958 via-pair route

Beside the partial-inductance method the tool shows Intel AN 958's via-pair loop, L = 10·h·ln(2s/D) pH with h (depth to the plane), s (via spacing) and D (via diameter) in mils — Johnson's (μ0/π)·H·ln(2S/D) with μ0/π = 10.16 pH/mil rounded. Unlike the partial form, which takes each via alone, the loop form counts the two vias' mutual inductance: closer vias, less loop. It adds the body ESL by case size — KYOCERA AVX's measured table (0603 870 pH, 0805 1050 pH, 1206 1200 pH, 1210 980 pH, unmounted, at 1 GHz), and for 0201 and 0402 AVX's own curve fit, labelled as such — to give the mounted ESL the plane pair sees: body + via pair + pads. That is the number the Cap Bank Analyzer's model ESL uses.

Insight. Above roughly 100 MHz the mounting inductance dominates the capacitor's effective impedance entirely. An 0402 with 0.6 nH of ESL mounted with two through-board vias easily picks up 2 nH of mounting inductance, tripling the high-frequency impedance the rail actually sees. The fixes, in order of effectiveness: put the plane pair closer to the surface (shorter vias), put the vias inside or immediately beside the pads (shorter loop), use more vias in parallel, and only then chase a lower-ESL capacitor.

Cap Bank Analyzer (Z(f))

Plots the impedance of a whole decoupling bank against frequency and finds the anti-resonance peaks.

Inputs. Build the capacitor table at the top: value, case size, count, and the ESL and ESR of each part. Tick a row's model boxes and its ESL comes from the case's body ESL plus the mounting set below (AN 958's via pair and the pad traces as microstrips), its ESR from the typical X5R/X7R law; untick them to type the datasheet's. Then set the sweep range (10 kHz to 1 GHz is typical), the target impedance, and optionally the regulator as a series R–L.

Outputs. A log-log |Z(f)| plot with your target line overlaid, plus worst-case |Z|, the NISM flatness ratio Z_max / Z_min, the count and location of anti-resonance peaks, and an overall pass/fail against the target.

Anti-resonance is the whole point. Two capacitors of different values in parallel do not simply add. Between their self-resonant frequencies, one is inductive while the other is still capacitive, and they form a parallel LC tank that produces an impedance peak — often higher than either capacitor alone. Adding a third value to "cover the gap" frequently creates two peaks instead of one.

Insight. The modern approach is fewer distinct values, not more. Many capacitors of the same value in parallel lowers impedance without creating new peaks, because they all resonate together. The NISM flatness ratio is the number to minimise: a flat PDN is a well-behaved PDN.

Typical ESR and the 1.57× rule

The model ESR is 11 mΩ·(C/1 µF)^−0.36, a least-squares fit to 103 Samsung X5R/X7R parts (0201–1210, 0 V, 25 °C) at self-resonance — within ×1.15 of every value's median, within ×2.3 of Würth's, inside Xilinx UG483's allowed windows, and clamped to its 10 nF–100 µF range. It is a typical part's, not yours, and constant with frequency, so anti-resonance peaks come out slightly high (conservative). Polymer and tantalum parts have no generic default.

A peak over the target is graded by Smith, Sandler and Bogatin's ringing rule: a resonant peak rings to about 0.64·ΔI·Z_peak, so a narrow peak up to ≈ 1.57× the target may still meet the ripple budget in the time domain (a note); beyond 1.57× it is a warning.

Plane pair, power pour, or none

Pick how the capacitors reach the load. No plane pair sums the bank as if every part sat at the load. The PCB Design Wizard never recommends a power plane — every plane is GND and power runs as wide traces or pours — so on its stackups there is no cavity unless a power pour sits over a GND plane, and the trace from each capacitor to the load adds inductance this mode does not see: keep the parts at the pins. Power plane over GND and power pour over a GND plane model the pair (or the pour's rectangle) as a cavity: enter its size, the dielectric between the two copper layers, Dk and Df, the load's position and via pairs, and each row's x, y from the corner.

What the plane pair adds

The cavity is the transmission-plane grid of the pair solved by its modes, the same model EyeOpener Lite checks against the exact modal expansion (median error 0.02–0.3 %, worst decile ≤ 1.1 %) and openEMS (resonances within 1.5–4.3 %). It reports each row's spreading inductance to the load — it adds to that row's ESL, and grows with distance as (μ0h/π)·ln(d/r) — the pair's own capacitance εA/h, its bare-cavity resonances, and |Z| at the load through the planes, drawn beside the lumped bank. It solves in the background on the desktop; in the browser press Solve plane pair. It assumes the GND plane is whole under the pair, the edges open, and the load's via pairs uncoupled to each other.

Buck / Boost Inductor

Inductor and output-capacitor sizing for a CCM switching converter.

Inputs. V_in, V_out, load current, switching frequency (500 kHz – 2 MHz for silicon, above 3 MHz for GaN), inductor ripple as a fraction of I_out (25–40 % is the usual choice), and the target output ripple voltage.

Outputs. Duty cycle, the required inductance, inductor ripple current ΔI_L, peak and RMS inductor current, average input current, and the minimum output capacitance with the maximum ESR that meets your ripple budget.

Insight. Size the inductor's saturation rating against the peak current, not the RMS or the average. A converter that saturates its inductor on a load step does not gently degrade — the inductance collapses, the current spikes and the switch usually dies. The 25–40 % ripple guideline is a compromise: less ripple needs a bigger inductor, more ripple needs a bigger output capacitor and costs efficiency in core loss.
