Signal integrity

Where the loss goes, what the stubs cost you, how much jitter you can afford, and whether the bus is length-matched.

Via Stub Impact (S21, eye and budget)

Quantifies how much of a high-speed channel's insertion-loss budget an open via stub eats, and tells you whether to back-drill.

Protocol preset

Pick from PCIe Gen3/4/5/6, USB 2.0 / 3.2 Gen2 / USB4 Gen3 / USB4 v2, 10G/25G-KR, 400GBASE-KR4, OIF CEI-56G / 112G, DisplayPort HBR3 / UHBR20, HDMI FRL, DDR4-3200, DDR5-6400/8400, LPDDR5-6400, SATA III, MIPI D-PHY 3.0, or Custom. Selecting a preset auto-fills the data rate, modulation, rise time, insertion-loss budget and FEC margin from the specification. Every field stays editable afterwards — the preset is a starting point, not a lock.

Inputs

Stub geometry. Stub length, drill / antipad / pad diameters, plating thickness, the used (signal-carrying) via length, differential via spacing, substrate εr, loss tangent tan δ, and a via-fill picker (Air / Epoxy / Conductive). The diagram labels every dimension.

Channel context. Main trace Z₀ (usually 50 Ω per leg), a differential checkbox for two-via analysis, data rate, modulation (NRZ / PAM4 / PAM3), rise time, insertion-loss budget at Nyquist, and FEC margin.

Method picker. Coaxial open-stub (Pozar §3.5) or Johnson lumped-L + stub (High-Speed Signal Propagation ch. 7). The Johnson form is more accurate above about 20 GHz.

Outputs

A live channel S21 plot leads the section: an amber Δ|S21| curve over log frequency, a dashed-blue marker at Nyquist, and a dotted-red marker at the quarter-wave resonance.

The numeric readouts cover via Z₀, used-barrel inductance, quarter-wave resonance frequency, stub Q, Nyquist frequency, insertion-loss adder at Nyquist and at 2 × Nyquist, notch depth, budget consumed (raw and after FEC credit), horizontal and vertical eye closure, PAM4 sub-eye, unit interval, and a colour-coded recommendation badge: OK / Watch / Back-drill recommended / Back-drill required.

S-param quality screens the swept stub network (the stub at a tee, Z₀ at both ports) for passivity, reciprocity, sampling and causality. A failing check appears as a message.

How a stub hurts

An unused via barrel below your signal layer is an open-circuited transmission line stub. At the frequency where the stub is a quarter wavelength long it presents a short at the signal layer and drops a deep notch in the channel response. Below resonance it just adds capacitance and loss; near resonance it can take 10 dB or more out of the link.

Insight. The number to watch is the quarter-wave resonance relative to your Nyquist frequency. If resonance sits above 2 × Nyquist the stub is usually harmless. As it approaches Nyquist the notch lands inside your signal band and back-drilling stops being optional. Back-drilling costs roughly +17 % at a Chinese fab and +30 % at a US fab — see the PCB Cost Estimator.

Where a stub really resonates

The quarter-wave frequency, c / (4·L·√εr), is where an unloaded stub resonates. A real stub passes planes, and their capacitance loads it.

Stub Measured or solved notch Quarter wave
Six-layer via, 1.7 mm 17.35 GHz (openEMS) 21.0 GHz
26-layer backplane, 264 mil, Dk 1 7.63 GHz (HFSS) 11.2 GHz
Same backplane as built 4.38 GHz (measured) 5.75 GHz

There the quarter wave reads 21–47 % high, and the full-wave via solve finds up to 72 % on dense GND-only stacks (a plane every 0.2–0.3 mm). So the tool also shows the lowest the stub may resonate, the quarter wave ÷ 1.75, as a marker on the plot. It warns when that lands below 2× Nyquist.

For the actual notch on your stack, the Via Properties tool's via network card solves it round the via's axis (full wave) and brackets it between two network estimates. A stub through many closely spaced planes can resonate below the ÷ 1.75 marker; the card says when it does.

Conductor Loss (Roughness)

Copper foil is not smooth. Its tooth profile lengthens the path skin-effect current must take, and above a few GHz that costs real dB.

Methodology dropdown. Hammerstad-Jensen, Hemispherical, or Cannonball-Huray (default). The Cannonball-Huray form — Simonovich 2015 — is the modern standard because it takes the datasheet Rz directly rather than requiring you to fit a surface-ratio parameter.

Inputs. Frequency, RMS roughness Δ for the Hammerstad and Hemispherical models, or datasheet Rz for Cannonball-Huray. Typical Rz values: STD foil 5.7 µm, RTF 4.4 µm, VLP 3.5 µm, HVLP 2.0 µm.

Outputs. The loss multiplier K versus smooth copper, the difference in dB, and the skin depth δ.

Insight. When the skin depth becomes comparable to the tooth height, the multiplier climbs fast. At 10 GHz the skin depth in copper is about 0.66 µm — smaller than the tooth on standard foil. That is why 25 Gbps designs specify VLP or HVLP foil: the laminate resin might be identical, but the copper roughness alone can be a 30–60 % loss difference.

Conductor Loss on a real line

K multiplies the loss of a surface, not of a line. The On a line section applies it to a cross-section: pick the topology and dimensions and the laminate's Dk and Df as the datasheet quotes them, and the 2D field solver gives the line's resistance per inch at DC, smooth and rough, the multiplier that results on this line, and the conductor loss αc = R/(2Z) in dB/in, smooth and rough. Z comes from the same solve with the laminate's Dk moved causally to the frequency (see Dielectric Dk/Df vs Frequency). For a pair it is the odd mode, the differential signal.

Planes as rough as the trace (on by default) applies K to the reference planes' faces too. Untick it to treat the planes as smooth: the line's multiplier then falls well below K, because on a stripline the planes carry roughly a fifth to a half of the loss. R itself is checked against Wheeler's rule (see Verification and limits); the roughness models are not.

Channel Insertion Loss Budget

Adds up every loss mechanism in a channel into one number you can compare against the specification's budget.

Line from. Two ways to describe the line:

  • Cross-section (2D field solver, causal laminate) — the default. Describe the line itself and let the solver find its loss.
  • Per-inch inputs — type Z₀, ε_eff and tan δ yourself, as earlier versions did. Nothing is moved with frequency, and R is the textbook ρ/(δ·2(w+t)).

Common inputs. Frequency (use Nyquist), trace length, the roughness model (Rz for Cannonball-Huray, RMS Δ for Hammerstad-Jensen), via stub length, and per-pair connector loss in dB.

Outputs. Total insertion loss with a per-component breakdown — dielectric, conductor, via stub, connector — plus α_d and α_c in dB/inch and the roughness multiplier K.

How to use it. The breakdown is the point. If dielectric dominates, a better laminate helps and wider traces do not. If conductor dominates, wider traces and smoother foil help and the laminate does not. Spending money on the wrong one is the most common channel-budget mistake.

Insight. Dielectric loss scales roughly linearly with frequency; conductor loss scales with the square root of frequency. So as data rates rise, dielectric loss becomes the dominant term — which is why the industry moved to low-Df laminates rather than just thicker copper.

Insertion loss from the cross-section

Pick the topology (any of the eight the Trace Impedance tool knows), W, T, H, h_top, s or s_b as the topology needs, and the laminate's Dk and Df as the datasheet quotes them, and at which frequency. The tool moves Dk and Df to your frequency with the Djordjevic–Sarkar model, solves the cross-section about a quarter of a second after you stop typing, and reports:

  • the line's impedance (Zdiff and Zodd for a pair) and its εeff and delay;
  • α_d from the field energy in each dielectric times its loss tangent;
  • α_c = R/(2Z), with R from the solver's surface current (smooth, then times K, blended with DC) — shown per line with its smooth and DC values.

Length, roughness, stub and connector edits re-cost the same solve instantly. Planes as rough as the trace works as in Conductor Loss.

Why the two modes differ

On the default 5 mil stripline (50.5 Ω, Dk 3.5) the per-inch formula gives a smooth conductor loss of 0.175 dB/in at 10 GHz and the cross-section 0.285 dB/in. ρ/(δ·2(w+t)) puts the current evenly round the strip and leaves out the planes; the real current crowds into the strip's edges and the planes carry their own share. The dielectric terms agree exactly when the frequency is the datasheet's — they diverge as you move away from it, because the per-inch mode holds Dk and Df flat.

Jitter Budget (Tj)

Total peak-to-peak jitter at a target bit error rate, using the Dual-Dirac model.

Inputs. Deterministic jitter Dj peak-to-peak in ps, random jitter Rj as an RMS value in ps, the baud rate, and the target BER (1e-9 / 1e-12 / 1e-15).

Outputs. Tj peak-to-peak at your BER, the unit interval, Tj as a percentage of the UI, and the Q factor the BER implies.

The model. Dual-Dirac separates jitter into a bounded deterministic part and an unbounded Gaussian part: Tj(BER) = Dj + Q(BER) × Rj, where Q is 12.7 at 1e-12 and 14.07 at 1e-15. The two parts must be kept separate because only the random part grows as you demand a lower error rate.

Insight. Random jitter is the expensive one. Halving Dj improves Tj by that amount once; halving Rj improves it by roughly 6.4 ps per ps at 1e-12, and by more at lower BER. If your budget is blown, attack the reference clock and the PLL before you attack the routing.

DDR Length Matching

Group skew analysis against JEDEC tolerances, with a per-signal trim recommendation.

Inputs. Pick the standard (DDR3 / DDR4 / DDR5 / LPDDR4 / LPDDR5) to load the JEDEC per-byte-lane tolerance, then fill the per-signal length table with the as-routed length of every DQ, DM, DQS, ADDR, CMD and CK signal. Set ε_eff for the propagation-velocity conversion.

Outputs. The JEDEC tolerance for the chosen standard, each signal's deviation from its group reference (DQS for DQ within a byte lane, CK for address and command), worst-case skew, shortest and longest signal, and a colour-coded in-spec / out-of-spec result.

The grouping is the important part. DDR is not matched to one global length. Each byte lane is matched to its own DQS strobe, and the address/command group is matched to CK. Byte lanes do not need to match each other — write levelling and read levelling absorb lane-to-lane skew. Trying to match everything to one number wastes routing area and serpentine length for no benefit.

Insight. Intra-pair skew on the differential strobes matters more than inter-signal skew within the lane, and it is the one thing training cannot fix. Match DQS+/DQS− to each other tightly, at the package pin if you can.
