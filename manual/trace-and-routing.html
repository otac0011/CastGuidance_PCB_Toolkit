Trace and routing

Sizing conductors: how wide for impedance, how wide for current, how far apart for voltage, and how long before the trace stops behaving like a wire.

Bandwidth and Max Length

Converts an edge rate into a frequency, and tells you how long a trace can be before you have to treat it as a transmission line.

Methodology dropdown. IPC-2251 knee frequency (BW = 0.35 / t_r), Howard Johnson (BW = 0.5 / t_r, more conservative), or direct frequency entry if you already know the number.

Inputs. Rise time t_r measured 20–80 %, the frequency if you chose direct entry, and ε_r_eff for the propagation velocity.

Outputs. Signal bandwidth (the knee frequency), the wavelength at that frequency, and the maximum conductor length before reflections matter — typically λ/6 of the knee.

Insight. The number that matters is the edge rate, not the clock rate. A 10 MHz clock from a modern FPGA with a 200 ps edge has a knee frequency near 1.75 GHz and needs the same care as a much faster bus. Ask what the driver's rise time is, not what the frequency on the schematic says.

Trace Impedance (openEMS 3D)

The main impedance tool. The inputs are on the left. The results are on the right: the line's impedance by the Clemens Method at the top, and every other method under it. The tool's details are under Show advanced: the 2D solver's card, fab-tolerance corners, width for a target and yield, and (on the desktop build) the 3D full-wave FDTD run with openEMS.

Results. The large number is Z₀ (Zdiff for a pair) by the Clemens Method, with εeff and the delay in ps/in under it; a pair also shows Zodd and Zcommon. Where the Clemens Method has no answer (solder mask is on, or the cross-section is outside its tables) the 2D field solver's answer takes its place, and an orange line says why.

Every method. Under the headline, a table lists the 2D field solver, Hammerstad-Jensen, IPC-2141, Cohn (1955) and Ghione & Naldi (1987), and the 3D FDTD answer once a run has returned. Each row shows its difference from the headline. A closed form that does not apply to the topology shows —.

Solve 2D. The Clemens Method and the closed forms update on every change. The 2D field solver runs only when you press Solve 2D under the table; after a change its row reads "press Solve 2D" until you press it again. Where the 2D solver is the headline (solder mask, or outside the Clemens Method's tables), the headline waits for the button too.

Topology. Pick from Standard Microstrip, Embedded Microstrip, Differential Microstrip, CPWG, Differential CPWG, Stripline, Edge-Coupled Differential Stripline, or Broadside-Coupled Differential Stripline. The dimensioned diagram and the visible input fields both change with the selection, so you can confirm you are describing the cross-section you meant.

Dielectric source. Where the Dk comes from. Whichever you pick, the same Dk drives the closed forms, the 2D field solver and the openEMS run, and the line under the picker says what it is and at what frequency.

  • Bundled material library (the default). The laminate library — Megtron, Rogers, Isola, AGC, ITEQ, FR-4 and others — with Dk and Df as the datasheet quotes them, or Custom for a manual Dk and Df. The Open datasheet ⇗ button opens the manufacturer's PDF. Resin content: below.
  • Laminate builder. Build the dielectric the way a fab does: pick the laminate family (29 of them, standard FR-4 to ultra-low-loss, each with the maker's own fitted resin), the glass it is sold in (only the grades that maker sells — Megtron 6 in low-Dk R-5775(N) or E-glass R-5775(K/G), for example), the glass style (106, 1078, 2116, 7628 … with its weight and usual resin range), the resin content and the number of plies. The resin content starts at the style's typical value. Tick Fit the resin content to a pressed thickness to do it the other way round: type the thickness you want and the tool finds the resin content that gives it. The card shows the thickness in mil and mm, the glass fraction, and the Dk and Df at the family's fit frequency, at 1 GHz and at 10 GHz. More resin makes a ply thicker and its Dk lower; a resin content outside the style's usual range, an estimated style, or a grade with no published construction table is flagged.
  • RF laminate catalog. Rogers RO4000/RO3000/RT-duroid, Taconic and a few datasheet-only materials, with the maker's design Dk (the one for impedance, not the process Dk) and the thicknesses each is sold in.
  • Custom Dk / Df. Type both.

Design frequency. The builder's and the catalog's Dk and Df are moved from the frequency they were fitted or quoted at to this one with the Djordjevic–Sarkar causal model (1 GHz by default). The 2D solver's dielectric loss is reported at it. Library and custom values are used as entered.

Use this dielectric's thickness as H. In the builder and catalog modes, tick it and H follows the built (or the sold) thickness.

Inputs. Trace width W, copper thickness T and substrate height H are always shown. The rest appear only when the topology needs them: cover height h_cover for embedded microstrip, top substrate h_top for any stripline (broadside included), edge spacing s for any differential variant, broadside spacing s_b for broadside-coupled, and coplanar gap g for CPWG variants. H is the laminate under the trace (its underside to the plane); h_top is the trace's top to the upper plane; a broadside pair is H, the lower trace, s_b, the upper trace and h_top, from the lower plane up.

Solder mask (open microstrips and CPWGs only, off by default). A conformal liquid mask of the given Dk, C1 thick on the bare laminate and C2 over the copper's top and walls (3.5, 0.8 mil and 0.5 mil by default). Only the 2D solver models it; the closed forms and the FDTD run do not. On the default 4.2 mil microstrip it takes about 3 Ω off Z₀.

2D field solver — the reference. A quasi-static finite-volume solve of the cross-section, run in-process (in the browser too) when you press Solve 2D (or Solve 2D now on its card). It shows Z₀ (Zdiff for a pair) and εeff; for a pair also Zodd, Zeven, Zcommon, one line's Z11 and the odd and even εeff; the delay in ps/in and the dielectric loss in dB/in at the design frequency (from the energy-weighted loss tangent; for copper loss use the Insertion Loss Budget, which solves the same cross-section). Below them, every closed form is listed with its difference from the 2D answer. Treat the 2D number as the reference for a uniform line's impedance: it is checked within 0.3 % against Cohn's exact stripline (see Verification and limits). A solve takes 17–400 ms on a desktop and about twice that in the browser; the status line shows the time and the grid size.

Analytic outputs. Four independent closed-form estimates — Cohn (1955), Hammerstad-Jensen, IPC-2141 and Ghione-Naldi. Only the methods that apply to the chosen topology produce a number; the rest show —. For the differential topologies the closed forms give Zdiff. The broadside-coupled formula is unreliable — see below.

3D FDTD section. Pick a Sim quality and press Run 3D Simulation. The solver reports Z₀ extracted from the FDTD field, which extraction method it used, its own analytic cross-check, and the mesh resolution. A library material is sent by name; any other dielectric source is sent as a custom Dk. Before you press Run, the card shows the mesh's cell count and the expected run time at each quality, warns above about two minutes, and disables Run when the mesh is over the solver's cell limit. See The 3D field solver for what the quality settings do and why this section is desktop-only.

Insight. Converge on a geometry with the Clemens Method — it answers while you type — then press Solve 2D to confirm with a solver as accurate as a commercial 2D solver for a uniform line, and keep the 3D run for what a cross-section cannot show: launches, discontinuities, dispersion. Running FDTD on every trial width is a waste of minutes.

Flex: cross-hatched ground plane

Flex circuits often hatch the ground plane: it keeps the circuit flexible, and on a thin film it raises an impedance that would otherwise be too low. Under Show advanced, tick The reference plane is a cross-hatch (microstrip lines: single, embedded, or an edge-coupled pair).

The hatch. Pick a preset or type your own:

  • Bar width and pitch: the pitch is centre to centre, so the opening is the pitch minus the bar.
  • Angle to the trace: 0° is a grid along the line, 45° a diamond, which is the common choice.
  • Shape: square/diamond, a rhombus stretched along the line, or triangles.

Every preset names its sources; see the presets. The card shows the copper share and the cell's length along the trace, and warns:

  • under 40 % copper (Polar);
  • a cell longer than 50 mil along the trace (Sierra Circuits);
  • bars or openings under 2–3 mil (what flex fabs build).

The stack. The line sits on the base film (H, at the dielectric's Dk), with the hatch under it. Coverlay over traces and coverlay under hatch are the adhesive-plus-film layers on each side; 1.5 mil at Dk 3.3 is 12.5 µm of polyimide over 25 µm of adhesive.

Solve over the hatch. The answer depends on where the line sits over the hatch, and a flex fab cannot control that to better than about ±50 µm. So the tool solves five positions across half a period of the pattern and gives the range (with the mean). A 0° grid is the most position-sensitive and 10–40° the least (Okayama's measurements).

The result appears on the card, as a row in the methods table, and in a note under the headline: the headline itself stays the solid-plane answer.

How the hatch is solved

Thin flex defeats the usual shortcuts.

  • 2D slices. On a 25 µm film over a 0.5 mm hatch, the 2D slice method of the literature (Sun et al., IEEE TSIPI 2024) reads the capacitance 13 % low.
  • Plain 3D electrostatics. Taking the inductance from the air capacitance, as for an ordinary line, reads it 15 % low. The return current must follow the bars, which makes the line slower and its impedance higher.

So the tool solves one period of the pattern in 3D, twice:

  • C: electrostatics with the dielectrics.
  • L: magnetostatics with the copper as a perfect conductor. The current circulating round every opening is found so that no flux passes through it.

Each is divided by the same solve over a solid plane on the same grid, so the grid's error cancels. The ratios then scale the 2D solver's exact solid-plane answer: Z = Z_solid·√(L ratio / C ratio).

It takes seconds per position on a desktop and longer in the browser, so it runs only when you press the button.

Resin content on library materials

A datasheet's Dk is for one construction. A thin, resin-rich prepreg (1080 or 106 at 65–75 % resin) reads lower than that; a glass-rich core (2116 or 7628 at 40–50 %) reads higher. The Resin content slider under the material moves the Dk for this.

  • It starts at the resin content the quoted Dk stands for, so until you move it the result is the datasheet's. Where the material matches a laminate-builder family, that is where the family's fitted model gives the quoted Dk (Megtron 6 48 %, 370HR 51 %); otherwise 50 %.
  • Away from it the Dk moves by the laminate builder's glass/resin mix, with the resin's own Dk solved so the mix passes through the quoted value.
  • Ten points of resin moves the Dk about 3–5 % on low-Dk glass and 6–9 % on E-glass FR-4. Megtron 6 goes from 3.45 at 48 % to 3.31 at 58 %.
  • Rogers, PTFE and FastRise materials are fixed constructions: the slider is greyed out and the note says why.
  • A moved resin content is sent to the openEMS run as a custom Dk.

For a full construction (glass style, plies, pressed thickness), use the laminate builder instead.

Fab-tolerance corners

The card under the 2D result solves the cross-section twice more, at the fab's two impedance corners, and shows the Z₀ range (Zdiff for a pair) beside the nominal. High Z puts every tolerance where it raises the impedance: Dk low, dielectrics thick, traces narrow and thin. Low Z is the reverse. Z₀ falls with Dk, width and copper and rises with height, so these two corners bound every mix of the four. Each corner's geometry is listed under the range.

Fab class. Standard order, impedance-controlled (the default) or RF-grade. The tolerances are typical values from a survey of US fab sheets and IPC-4101/4562/6012:

Class Width outer / inner Prepreg / core Dk Inner foil
Standard ±1.0 / ±0.75 mil ±15 % / ±10 % ±0.20 −10 / +5 %
Controlled ±0.5 / ±0.4 mil ±10 % / ±5 % ±0.10 −10 / +5 %
RF-grade ±0.3 / ±0.25 mil ±10 % / ±3 % ±0.05 −10 / +5 %

How the corners move the geometry

  • Dielectrics. Every dielectric (H, h_top, h_cover, s_b) is taken as prepreg, the wider tolerance, unless you tick Dielectrics are cores. Each has a floor: on a thin layer the floor wins. On a 2.1 mil prepreg the ±0.5 mil floor is ±24 %.
  • Copper. Outer plated copper moves by its weight's band (½ / 1 / 2 oz). Inner foil moves −10 / +5 %.
  • Etch. The microstrips and CPWGs are outer, plated traces. The embedded microstrip and the striplines are inner foil. A pair's gap and a coplanar gap lose what the trace gains, because the pitch is kept.

Reading it. Every tolerance sits at its limit at once. That is a worst case, not a statistic: a real board lies inside it. A controlled-impedance fab also trims the widths toward the target, so what it delivers spreads less. No fab publishes its etch as a ± figure, so the width tolerance is the least certain number here.

Speed. The corners solve when you press Solve the corners, off the UI thread on the desktop and in a few tenths of a second in the browser. They reuse the nominal answer when it is current.

Width for a target, and yield

A card under the corners answers two questions without you iterating by hand.

Find the width. Enter the target (Z₀, or Zdiff for a pair) and press Find the width. The tool searches the width by the Clemens Method, then solves that width on the real 2D field solver. If the solver disagrees, it takes the difference out once and solves again. It sets W to the answer and shows the solver's impedance there, usually within 0.2 % of the target in one or two solves. Solder mask is not in the Clemens Method's tables: untick it, search, then re-tick.

Yield. It simulates 2,000 builds on the fab class picked above, with each tolerance taken as ±3σ and independent. It reports the share within your band (±10 % by default) around the target, the mean, σ, and the 1st–99th percentile range. The corners are a worst case; the yield is how often it happens.

The Clemens Method

The Clemens Method gives a line's impedance from pre-solved 2D field solutions. It is named for the toolkit's author, as the Hammerstad–Jensen formulas are named for theirs. It appears as Z₀ — Clemens Method beside the closed forms, and in the 2D card's comparison lines.

How it works. It is a table of real 2D field solutions, not a formula fitted to them:

  • For each topology the 2D field solver was run at about 300 to 10,000 cross-sections (25,526 in all).
  • The table stores two things. One is Z_air, the line's impedance with the dielectric replaced by air, which depends only on the geometry's ratios. The other is the filling factor q = (εeff − 1)/(εr − 1).
  • Because Z₀ = Z_air/√εeff exactly, the hard-to-fit part never has to be fitted.
  • Between points it interpolates in ln of each ratio.

How good it is.

  • Against real solves at random points it is within 0.25 % of the 2D solver (validation).
  • FDTD spot checks on typical geometries agree with it as closely as they agree with the solver.

What it is for. It is instant, so it drives everything that needs many solves: the width search, the yield, the Differential Pair Designer's surface and the wizard's pair widths. The 2D card's reference value is always a real solve.

Glass-weave skew card

For the differential topologies a card estimates the intra-pair skew from the glass weave. It takes the laminate builder's glass style when the builder is the dielectric source. Otherwise you pick the style on the card. See Glass-weave skew under the Differential Pair Designer. The card is the same in both tools.

Broadside-coupled pairs: use the 2D result

For Broadside-Coupled Differential Stripline the 2D card is the result, not just the reference: its title says so. Like every topology it solves when you press Solve 2D; the Clemens Method's headline is built from the same solver. The Cohn-type closed form in the Cohn row is marked unreliable: it uses only W and s_b and never sees the planes or H and h_top, and reads about 48 % high on the default geometry (99.2 Ω against the 2D solver's 66.9 Ω). The 2D odd mode agrees with the exact midplane identity to 0.02 %. The closed form stays on the page only for comparison.

IPC-2141 against the 2D solver

The IPC-2141 row is a simplified fit, and on thin modern dielectrics it reads low: 11.4 % on the default microstrip (44.6 Ω against 50.3 Ω), 10.9 % on the default embedded microstrip, 9 % on the default pair, and 4–9 % across w/h 0.5–2 on 5 mil FR-4. Hammerstad–Jensen is within 0.1 % of the 2D solver on the same lines. When the IPC-2141 row is more than 5 % off the 2D answer the 2D card shows a red warning saying by how much. The formula is kept as it was for parity with the original toolkit; use the 2D or Hammerstad–Jensen value (validation).

Differential Pair Designer

Not just a Z_diff calculator: a manufacturability finder. You name a target differential impedance and it sweeps a two-dimensional grid of trace widths and edge-to-edge spacings, showing every combination that lands inside your tolerance band.

Inputs. Structure (edge-coupled microstrip or edge-coupled embedded microstrip), target Z_diff and tolerance, substrate εr, frequency, copper thickness T, substrate height H, cover height h_cover for the embedded variant, and the W and S sweep ranges that bound the search.

Outputs.

  • A 3D isometric surface of Z_diff over (W, S), with a green band filling every cell within ±tolerance of your target.
  • A recommended W and S — the most manufacturable point in the band. Wider trace and more spacing wins ties, because both are easier to fabricate and both are less sensitive to etch variation.
  • Z_diff, Z_common, Z_odd and Z_even at the recommendation, plus the W/H and S/H ratios.
  • Diagnostics for "no cell hits the band — widen the sweep" and "the recommendation is sitting on the edge of the swept range".
  • A standing validation warning: this closed form reads Zdiff high (see below). Confirm the recommended W and S in the Trace Impedance tool's 2D solver before you commit a stackup.

Why a surface instead of a single answer. There is no unique (W, S) for a given Z_diff; there is a whole valley of them. Seeing the valley tells you which end of it is robust. A solution at the narrow-trace end will drift with etch tolerance far more than one at the wide end.

Insight. Common targets: 90 Ω for USB 2.0, 100 Ω for Ethernet, PCIe Gen3+ and HDMI, 120 Ω for CAN-FD. If the green band is empty, the usual fix is a thinner dielectric to the reference plane, not a different trace width.

How far to trust the designer's surface

For edge-coupled microstrip and symmetric edge-coupled stripline the surface comes from the Clemens Method, within 0.25 % of the 2D field solver. The line under the recommendation says so.

The other structures (embedded, asymmetric, broadside) still use the designer's closed form: a Hammerstad–Jensen single line and a coupling term 0.347·e^(−2.655·s/h). Against the 2D field solver it reads Zdiff 8–15 % high on microstrip pairs, because that coupling term is nearly zero at ordinary spacings (validation). For those structures, confirm the numbers in Trace Impedance.

Fab-tolerance corners (designer)

The corners card takes the recommended (W, S) to the fab's High Z and Low Z corners. It uses the same fab classes and tolerances as Trace Impedance, computed with the same model as the sweep (the Clemens Method where it covers the structure). It shows:

  • the Zdiff range;
  • each tolerance alone at its limits, so you can see which one dominates (on a 4 mil prepreg it is usually the height);
  • whether the corners stay inside your target band.

The open microstrip is an outer, plated trace. The embedded one is inner foil. For the 2D field solver's corners, use Trace Impedance.

Glass-weave skew

Glass cloth is bundles of glass (Dk about 6) woven with resin (about 3) between them. A pair routed along the weave can put one leg over a bundle and the other over resin for its whole length. That skew cannot be removed by matching the lengths. The card multiplies the glass style's measured skew rate by the length that runs along the weave. It is a literature-based estimate, not a field solve.

Inputs.

  • Pair length in inches.
  • Routing angle. 0° / 90° (the whole length counts along the weave), 10° or more, zig-zag at 10–13°, or artwork rotated 10° on the panel. The last three count no length along the weave.
  • Data rate in GT/s. For PAM4, enter the baud rate.
  • Glass style. Picked here, or taken from the laminate builder in Trace Impedance.
  • Rate basis. Measured per style (the default), or Intel's conservative 16.3 ps/in.

The warning rules. The card warns, and never fails, when either holds:

  • 4 in or more runs along the weave above 5 GT/s (Kuchy & Loyer 2008; Cadence 2016);
  • the skew is over 20 % of a UI (Bogatin 2017).

Mitigated routing is judged on the one measured residual, 0.2 ps/in (Gore 2017).

Glass-weave mitigations

  • Angle routing. Route at 10° or more to the edges, or zig-zag. Zig-zag's benefit has no published measurement.
  • Rotated artwork. Have the fab rotate the artwork 10° (about a 10 % cost adder) and say so on the fab drawing.
  • Spread glass. Spread 1080 measured ΔDk 0.14 against 0.50 for standard 1080. It is not a guarantee: spread 1067 still measured 6.5 ps/in.
  • A lower-rate style, such as 2116 or 1035.

Bundle pitch. When the style's count is published, the card also gives the bundle pitch next to the pair pitch W + S. The worst case is near half the bundle pitch.

Parallel Jumper Impedance

When a differential pair passes through two zero-ohm jumpers side by side, the two parts form a short coupled line. The pair's impedance through them depends on how far apart they sit. Name the target (90, 95, 100 Ω, any number) and the tool returns the centre-to-centre pitch that gives it, and says whether that spacing is buildable.

Inputs.

  • Target Z_diff and tolerance.
  • Part: an 0603, 0805 or 1206 zero-ohm jumper (Yageo RC outlines, editable under Show advanced).
  • Distance to the GND plane under the parts: the input that matters most.
  • εr of that dielectric.
  • The lands: pad length, pad width, and the gap between one part's two pads.
  • Your minimum pad-to-pad gap between the two parts (default 5 mil).

Outputs.

  • The pitch, and the matching gaps between the bodies and between the pads, in the selected unit.
  • The pitch window for your tolerance.
  • A verdict against your minimum pad gap, and the impedance you get at that gap.
  • The reachable range: from parts at the minimum gap to parts far apart.
  • A curve of Z_diff against pitch.

Under Show advanced: the three sections and the end-face inductance.

How the jumper calculator works

No field solver runs; the result updates as you type. The footprint is three coupled-line sections in series:

  • the pad beyond the part;
  • the pad, solder and end cap;
  • the jumper film on top of the ceramic body.

Each section comes from Garg & Bahl's coupled-microstrip capacitances. The gap dielectric between the films was fitted once to 2D field solves. The one truly 3D effect is the current climbing the end faces of the part. It is computed from partial-element (PEEC) inductance of the end-cap walls, with their images in the GND plane and the neighbouring part.

The result is the section impedance: the characteristic impedance of a uniform line that behaves like the footprint, at low frequency. A 30 ps TDR can't fully resolve a 1–3 mm section, so it shows a smaller excursion.

How far to trust the jumper calculator

The model was checked against 3D full-wave (openEMS) runs of the same two jumpers on boards 0.1 to 1.575 mm from GND, εr 3.5 to 4.3:

  • 0603, 0805 and 1206 on a solid plane: within 1.6 %. On a 62 mil board that is a few hundredths of a millimetre of pitch.
  • A plane void under the pads: the formula reads about 8 % high (four 3D runs, 5.5 to 10.9 %). Ticking the void box divides that out.
  • Frequency: the footprint stops being a short lumped section in the tens of GHz. Its impedance then rises: for 0603 on 1.6 mm, +7 % at 10 GHz and +20 % at 15 GHz.
  • Not measured: no coupon yet (gaps).

  • Closer means lower impedance. The two films couple strongly, so moving the parts together lowers Z_diff. On a 62 mil board each 0.1 mm of pitch is worth about 9 Ω.
  • With the plane close, pitch stops mattering. On 0.2 mm to GND, 0805 and 1206 jumpers cannot reach 100 Ω at any spacing. The tool says so and suggests voiding the plane under the parts.
  • Neighbouring lanes. Jumpers of different lanes at a similar pitch couple just as strongly: the 3D runs showed −15 to −20 dB near-end crosstalk at 20 GHz.

Conductor Properties

Trace current capacity, resistance, voltage drop and skin depth.

Methodology dropdown. IPC-2152 (2009, modern empirical fit) or IPC-2221 (1998, the legacy K · ΔT^0.44 · A^0.725 curve, from 1950s still-air data). They do not differ by a fixed factor. On an outer layer with no plane nearby IPC-2152 is the more conservative: 100 mil of 1 oz copper carries 3.3 A at ΔT 10 °C by IPC-2152 against 4.7 A by IPC-2221. On an inner layer IPC-2221 halves the current, an assumption IPC-2152's measurements overturned, so there IPC-2152 gives about 1.4–1.5× as much. A nearby plane raises the IPC-2152 figure further. Use IPC-2152 unless a customer specification names IPC-2221 (validation).

Inputs. Width W, thickness T, length L, current I, allowed temperature rise ΔT, ambient T_a, frequency (for skin depth), distance to the nearest plane (IPC-2152 only), and a parallel count for paralleled power feeds.

Outputs. Cross-section, the recommended width for your current, DC resistance, voltage drop, power dissipated (in W and dBm), skin depth, the skin-depth-to-thickness ratio, and the "virtual" ΔT the formula was calibrated against.

Insight. ΔT of 10 °C is conservative, 20 °C is normal, 30 °C is aggressive but common in power sections. The plane distance input matters a lot in IPC-2152 — a trace over a nearby plane runs much cooler than one in free space because the plane is a heatsink.

Fusing Current

The current at which a trace melts, via Onderdonk's adiabatic equation.

Methodology dropdown. Onderdonk 1928 (adiabatic, conservative), or Onderdonk × a user multiplier when you want to apply your own derating.

Inputs. Width W, thickness T, fault duration t, ambient T_a, the multiplier if you chose the advisory method, and optionally a steady-state current for cross-checking against the ampacity tool.

Outputs. Fusing current at your chosen duration, the raw Onderdonk value, and the cross-section in both mil² and circular mils.

Adiabatic means the model assumes no heat escapes during the fault — all the energy goes into raising the copper's temperature to its 1085 °C melting point. That is true for short faults and pessimistic for long ones, which is the right direction for a safety calculation.

Insight. A trace survives 5–10× its steady-state ampacity for a few milliseconds. Use this tool to size an upstream fuse so the fuse blows before the trace does — or deliberately, to design a sacrificial fuse trace on a cost-sensitive board.

Min Conductor Spacing

Minimum conductor-to-conductor spacing for a working voltage.

Methodology dropdown. IPC-2221C Table 6-1 — a single lookup on voltage and environment, appropriate for board-level signal work — or IEC 60664-1 Ed.3, which accounts for pollution degree, material CTI and insulation type, and is what you need for mains-referenced safety compliance.

Inputs. Peak voltage (note: 230 Vac is 325 V peak), plus either the IPC environment class or the IEC pollution degree, CTI grade and insulation type.

Outputs. A single minimum spacing for IPC. For IEC, creepage (measured along the surface) and clearance (measured through air) are reported separately because they are different requirements with different numbers.

Insight. Creepage is always ≥ clearance. If you cannot make the creepage distance fit, milling a slot in the board between the high-voltage and low-voltage sections gives effectively infinite creepage in a few millimetres of board area. This is standard practice and costs almost nothing.
