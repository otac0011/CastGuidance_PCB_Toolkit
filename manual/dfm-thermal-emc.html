Paste Aperture (IPC-7525)

Checks whether solder paste will actually release from a stencil aperture.

Inputs. Aperture shape (square, rectangular, circular), the relevant dimensions, and the stencil thickness — 4 mil (0.10 mm) for general work, 3 mil for fine pitch.

Outputs. Area ratio, aspect ratio, aperture area, and the IPC-7525B verdict (pass / marginal / fail).

The two ratios.

  • Area ratio = aperture floor area ÷ aperture wall area. For a circle that reduces to D / (4T). This is the number that predicts release: paste sticks to the walls and releases from the floor, so the floor has to win. IPC-7525B sets the floor at 0.66.
  • Aspect ratio = aperture width ÷ stencil thickness, minimum 1.5. Relevant for long thin apertures where the width, not the area, is the constraint.

Insight. Below 0.66 area ratio you have three levers: a thinner foil, a larger aperture, or a nano-coated or electroformed stencil that releases better than laser-cut stainless. On a mixed board where fine-pitch parts need 3 mil and connectors need 5 mil, a step stencil — locally thinned over the fine-pitch area — is the standard answer.

PCB Cost Estimator

A fabrication-cost model with regional pricing, per-feature multipliers and the current US tariff stack. It is for comparing options — "what does six layers cost versus four", "what does switching to Rogers do" — before you go to a real quote.

The model

Each cost factor carries both a raw multiplier (what the reference data says, e.g. ×5.00 for a Tachyon 100G laminate) and a cost share (the fraction of board cost that factor actually affects — about 20 % for a prototype, since laminate is a small part of small-quantity board cost).

The effective multiplier is 1 + (raw − 1) × share. So Tachyon 100G on a 4-layer 5-piece prototype multiplies the board by ×1.80, not ×5.00. The same laminate on a 12-layer 1000-piece order has a higher share — material amortises more of the total once setup is spread — giving ×2.80.

The breakdown table shows all three columns (Raw, Share, Effect) so you can see the reasoning rather than trusting a single number.

Input groups

Order. Region (China DDP, JLCPCB/PCBWay class, or USA, 4PCB/Sierra/OSH Park class), quantity, and board size. The 5-piece prototype is the baseline anchor. Area scaling starts above 100 × 100 mm.

Stackup. Layer count, board thickness, outer and inner copper weight.

Fabrication. Trace/space, material (the same laminate library as the impedance tool, each carrying a board-cost factor), surface finish, soldermask colour, silkscreen.

Vias and drill. Via finish, via-in-pad, minimum drill, PTH aspect ratio, drill density.

HDI and microvias. HDI build (None / 1+N+1 / 2+N+2 / 3+N+3 / any-layer ELIC), laser microvia diameter, microvia stacking, backdrilling.

Signal integrity. Controlled impedance and its tolerance.

Mechanical. Castellated holes, edge plating.

Quality and turnaround. IPC class, lead time.

Add-ons. Stencil, construction type (rigid FR-4, aluminium MCPCB, copper-core, flex, rigid-flex, ceramic), and a US tariff toggle.

Outputs

A hero block with total order price, per-piece price and the multiplier/fee breakdown, followed by a table of every factor that contributed. Warnings flag combinations the reference data explicitly calls out — AS9100 with a Chinese fab, ceramic substrates being quote-only, sub-MOQ orders.

Insight. Several things that feel expensive are free at Chinese proto fabs and expensive in the US: controlled impedance, ENIG on 6+ layers, POFV/VIPPO, non-green soldermask, both-sides silkscreen. Several things are expensive everywhere: layer count, exotic laminate, tight drill, HDI. The breakdown table is there so you can find which knob is actually costing you.

Engineering-grade, not quote-grade. Typically within ±25 % of a real quote. Always verify on the fab's own quote page before purchasing.

Thermal Management

Junction temperature with and without a heatsink.

Inputs. Ambient T_a, dissipated power P, θJA from the package datasheet, and for the heatsinked case θJC (junction-to-case), θCS (case-to-sink, the thermal interface) and θSA (sink-to-ambient, from the heatsink datasheet). Plus Tj_max from the datasheet — typically 105, 125 or 150 °C.

Outputs. Tj without a heatsink, Tj with one, and the margin to Tj_max in each case.

Insight. θJA is not a property of the package — it is a property of the package on the test board JEDEC specified, usually a 2s2p board in still air. Your board is not that board. Treat θJA as a comparison figure between packages, and use θJC plus your own board's spreading path when the margin is tight.

Thermal Via Array

Total thermal resistance from an SMT exposed pad down to the plane below.

Inputs. Via count, hole diameter, plating thickness, via height to the plane, exposed-pad width and length, via-fill thermal conductivity (0 for air-filled), and solder void fraction (5–15 % for a well-designed aperture).

Outputs. Single-via θ, the array θ including the void penalty, the spreading resistance θ of the pad, the spreading fraction, and total θ — which is the number your thermal model needs.

What the spreading term is. It is Holm's exact constriction resistance 1/(4·k·a) of a round contact of the pad's area on a half-space of copper — the limit of copper much thicker than the pad. A 1 oz plane is the opposite limit: heat leaving a 2 mm-radius pad through 35 µm of copper to a rim 20 mm away sees about 27 °C/W, against 0.3 °C/W from the half-space term. Treat the spreading number as a lower bound (validation).

Insight. Adding vias has diminishing returns fast, because once the array resistance drops below the spreading resistance of the plane it lands on, more vias change nothing. The tool shows both terms so you can see which one is dominating. When spreading dominates, the fix is more copper area or a thicker plane, not more holes.

Radiated Emissions — Small Loop

Predicted E-field from a small current loop, compared against FCC and CISPR limits.

Inputs. Frequency (the harmonic of interest — n × switching frequency), loop length and width, peak loop current at that harmonic, and the reporting distance (3 m for FCC/CISPR, 10 m for some standards).

Outputs. Loop area, predicted E-field in µV/m and dBµV/m, the applicable standard limit (FCC Class A/B, CISPR Class A/B), the margin, and a colour-coded pass/fail.

Insight. Emission scales with loop area and with the square of frequency. Halving the loop area buys 6 dB; the harmonic content of your edge rate is what sets the frequency. This is the quantitative argument for tight return paths: it is not a rule of thumb, it is a squared term.

A first-order screen, not a compliance prediction. The model assumes a simple electrically-small loop in free space. Real boards have cables, enclosures and ground structures that dominate real emissions, and a test site's ground reflection can add up to 6 dB. Use it to compare two layouts and to find which harmonic is your problem — not to decide you will pass.

Emissions limits and the near field

The FCC Class B limits match 47 CFR §15.109(a), and the FCC Class A limits match §15.109(b): 90 / 150 / 210 / 300 µV/m (39.1 / 43.5 / 46.4 / 49.5 dBµV/m) at 10 m. Versions before 2026-10-20 used a Class A table about 10 dB higher (the 3 m equivalents labelled 10 m), so their Class A margins were about 10 dB optimistic: re-check any Class A result from an older version. The field is the loop's far-field term only: close in (kr below about 2.5, e.g. 3 m at 30 MHz) the exact field is up to 1.1 dB higher, and the tool adds a note (validation).
