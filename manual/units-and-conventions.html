Units and conventions

Everything is SI underneath

Every calculation in the toolkit is done in SI base units — metres, amperes, volts, hertz, farads, henries. The unit you see on a field is a display convention applied on the way in and on the way out. This matters for one practical reason: switching a unit selector never changes the design, it only re-renders the same physical geometry in different numbers.

Length

Unit Exact value Where you see it
mil 1 mil = 0.0254 mm exactly = 25.4 µm Default for most PCB geometry fields
mm — Package drawings, laminate thicknesses, drill sizes
µm — Copper plating, microvia geometry, surface roughness
inch 1 inch = 25.4 mm exactly Trace lengths in loss budgets

A mil is one thousandth of an inch, not a millimetre. It is the dominant unit in North American PCB fabrication and in IPC's older standards, so most fields default to it. IPC's newer standards and every package drawing you will get from a semiconductor vendor are metric.

Most length outputs are shown in both: 9.45 mil (0.240 mm).

Tools with a unit selector

The BGA Pad Designer has a single Units dropdown at the top of the page that governs every input and every output at once, so you can work entirely in mm or entirely in mils. Switching it converts what is displayed; it does not reinterpret what you typed. If you enter 0.30 mm and switch to mils you get 11.81 mil, not 0.30 mil.

Copper weight and thickness

Copper is specified by weight per unit area, not by thickness, because that is how foil is made and sold.

Copper weight Thickness
0.5 oz 0.689 mil ≈ 17.5 µm
1 oz 1.378 mil ≈ 35.0 µm
2 oz 2.756 mil ≈ 70.0 µm
3 oz 4.134 mil ≈ 105 µm

"1 oz copper" means a sheet whose mass is one ounce per square foot. IPC-2152 quotes 1.378 mil; the toolkit uses that exact value everywhere rather than the rounded 1.4 mil you sometimes see.

Finished copper is thicker than base copper on outer layers. Plating adds roughly 1 oz to whatever base foil you started with, so a board ordered as "1 oz outer" typically finishes around 1.4–2 oz. Where a tool asks for copper thickness it wants the finished value unless the field says otherwise.

Dielectric constant

Three related numbers appear across the toolkit and they are not interchangeable.

Dk (or ε_r) — the bulk relative permittivity of the laminate, from the manufacturer's datasheet. Always quoted at a stated frequency, usually 10 GHz; some high-speed materials publish at 28 GHz. Dk falls slowly with frequency.

ε_eff (effective dielectric constant) — what a microstrip trace actually sees. Part of the field is in the laminate and part is in the air above it, so ε_eff lands between 1 and Dk. A rough starting point is ε_eff ≈ 0.65 × Dk for surface microstrip, but the real value depends on the width-to-height ratio — compute it with Er Effective.

Dk for stripline — a stripline trace is buried in laminate on all sides, so ε_eff equals Dk. This is why stripline is slower than microstrip on the same material.

Getting these confused is the single most common source of a wrong impedance or a wrong propagation delay. If a field asks for ε_eff and you give it bulk Dk, your trace will come out too narrow.

Loss tangent

Df, also written tan δ, is the dielectric loss tangent — the fraction of energy the laminate absorbs per cycle. Like Dk it is quoted at a stated frequency and, unlike Dk, it generally rises with frequency. Typical values: 0.02 for generic FR-4, 0.005 for a mid-loss material, 0.002 for Megtron-class, 0.0009 for PTFE.

Use Dielectric Dk/Df vs Frequency to move a datasheet point to your design frequency rather than assuming the datasheet number applies everywhere.

Frequency, rise time and bandwidth

High-speed digital work needs a frequency to plug into an RF formula, and what you have is a rise time. The two standard conversions:

  • Knee frequency, IPC-2251: BW = 0.35 / t_r
  • Knee frequency, Howard Johnson: BW = 0.5 / t_r (more conservative)

Both are in Bandwidth & Max Length.

Nyquist frequency for a serial link is half the baud rate — 8 GHz for a 16 Gbps NRZ link. For PAM4 the symbol rate is half the bit rate, so a 32 Gbps PAM4 link has the same 8 GHz Nyquist as a 16 Gbps NRZ one. Loss budgets are almost always quoted at Nyquist.

Impedance conventions

  • Z₀ — single-ended characteristic impedance, one conductor against its reference plane. Usually 50 Ω.
  • Z_diff — differential impedance of a pair. Roughly 2 × Z_odd, and less than 2 × Z₀ because the coupling between the traces pulls it down.
  • Z_odd / Z_even — the impedance of one trace when the pair is driven differentially / in common mode.
  • Z_common — the pair driven as one conductor, roughly Z_even / 2.

Common targets: 90 Ω differential for USB 2.0, 100 Ω for Ethernet, PCIe and HDMI, 120 Ω for CAN-FD, 85 Ω for some DDR4 implementations.

Decibels

  • dB is a ratio. 10 dB is 10× power, 20 dB is 10× voltage.
  • dBm is an absolute power referenced to 1 mW. 0 dBm = 1 mW.
  • Insertion loss is quoted as a positive number of dB in this toolkit ("3.2 dB of loss"), not as a negative S21.

The dBm Reference Chart converts between dBm, mW and Vrms at a chosen system impedance — remember that a dBm value only maps to a voltage once you fix the impedance.

Temperature

Ambient and rise are in °C throughout. Where a formula needs absolute temperature the tool converts internally. ΔT always means rise above ambient, not an absolute temperature: ΔT = 10 °C with T_a = 25 °C means the copper reaches 35 °C.

Tolerances and the min / typ / max convention

Where a tool reports a min / typical / max triplet, the middle column is the design target and the outer columns are a design window, not a manufacturing measurement. They answer "what range of values is acceptable", not "what will the fab actually deliver". Where a geometric limit is tighter than the standard's window, the reported maximum is clamped to the real limit and a diagnostic says so.
