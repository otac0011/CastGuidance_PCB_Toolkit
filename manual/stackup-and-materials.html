Stackup and materials

The first decisions on a board: what laminate, how many layers, how thick, and whether the copper balance will let it come out flat.

Material Comparison

A side-by-side browser for the bundled PCB laminate library — Panasonic Megtron, Rogers, Isola, AGC, ITEQ, Elite and generic FR-4 among others. It performs no calculation; it exists so you can compare candidates on one screen instead of opening a dozen datasheet PDFs.

Filter row. The filter box does a substring match against family, vendor and product name. Sort by orders on Vendor, Product, Tg, Dk, Df or z-CTE. Compare selected only collapses the table to just the rows you have ticked.

Columns. Checkbox, Family, Vendor, Product, Tg (°C), Dk, Df, z-CTE, spec frequency (GHz), and an amber Open ⇗ link that hands the manufacturer's datasheet to your browser.

What the columns mean.

  • Tg — glass transition temperature. Above Tg the resin softens and z-axis expansion accelerates sharply. Lead-free assembly wants Tg ≥ 150 °C; multiple reflows or thick boards want 170 °C or better.
  • Dk / Df — see Units and conventions. Both are quoted at the frequency in the Spec f column, which is why that column exists.
  • z-CTE — expansion through the thickness, in ppm/°C. This is what cracks plated barrels during thermal cycling. Low z-CTE matters far more than x/y-CTE for via reliability.

Insight. Do not compare Dk across two materials whose spec frequencies differ without first moving them to a common frequency. A material quoted at 28 GHz will look worse than one quoted at 10 GHz even when it is better.

Dielectric Dk/Df vs Frequency

Laminate datasheets give you Dk and Df at one frequency. Your design is at a different one. This tool moves the datasheet point using the Djordjevic-Sarkar causal wideband Debye model, which is the standard way to extrapolate a single measured point into a physically consistent curve.

Inputs. Pick a material from the dropdown to auto-fill datasheet Dk, Df and spec frequency, or choose Custom to type your own. Then set the target frequency where you want to know the values.

Outputs. Dk and Df at the target frequency, the change in Dk versus the datasheet, and the fitted ε_∞ and Δε parameters that define the model.

Exact and approximate fits

By default the fit is exact at the datasheet point: ask for the datasheet's own frequency and you get its Dk and Df back, whatever the corners m₁ and m₂. Untick Exact fit for the approximation earlier versions used, which assumes the corners are far apart and drifts from the datasheet point when they are not. The line under the outputs always shows both, at the target and at the datasheet point. The same exact fit moves the laminate in the Trace Impedance tool's picker and in the Insertion Loss Budget and Conductor Loss tools' line sections, so all three agree with this page at the default corners (10 kHz and 1 THz there; this page's defaults are 10³ and 10¹⁵ rad/s). A warning appears if the fit needs ε∞ below 1 — a loss too high for corners that close.

Why "causal" matters. A model that lets Dk stay flat while Df is non-zero is physically impossible — it implies a response before the stimulus. The Djordjevic-Sarkar form ties the two together correctly, which is why it is what serious channel simulators use. A flat-Dk assumption will make your simulated channel too fast and too lossless.

Insight. Dk falls with frequency and Df usually rises. Between 1 GHz and 10 GHz a typical FR-4 loses 2–4 % of its Dk, which shifts a 50 Ω trace by roughly 1 Ω — small. Over the same span Df can nearly double, which is not small at all in a loss budget.

Er Effective

Computes the effective dielectric constant a microstrip trace sees, using the Hammerstad-Jensen static solution plus the Kirschning-Jansen dispersion correction. There is no methodology dropdown; this is the accepted pair.

Inputs. Trace width W, height H to the reference plane, bulk substrate εr, and the frequency of interest.

Outputs. ε_eff at DC (no dispersion), ε_eff at your frequency, and the W/H ratio.

Insight. A low W/H ratio puts more of the field in the air above the trace and pulls ε_eff toward 1; a high W/H ratio buries the field in the laminate and pulls ε_eff toward the bulk Dk. This is why a narrow microstrip is faster than a wide one on the same board. For stripline you do not need this tool at all — the trace is surrounded by laminate, so ε_eff = Dk.

Stackup Symmetry and Bow

Predicts whether a stackup will warp, and checks the result against the IPC-6012E bow and twist limit for your board size.

Inputs. A table with one row per layer, each carrying that layer's copper weight and coverage as a percentage, or a preset to fill it (below). Plus the board length, which sets the IPC limit.

Outputs. Layer count, a symmetry score, a colour-coded warp risk (low / medium / high), and the IPC-6012E bow limit in absolute terms for your board.

How to read it. Boards warp because copper and resin shrink differently as the laminate cools from lamination temperature. A stackup that is a mirror image about its centreline shrinks evenly and comes out flat. One that is copper-heavy on top and sparse on the bottom curls. The symmetry score measures how well layer n from the top matches layer n from the bottom.

What to do about a bad score. Add copper thieving (non-functional copper pours) to the sparse layers to balance coverage, or restack so the heavy planes sit symmetrically about the centre. Both are free changes at design time and expensive after the first article comes back bowed.

Insight. IPC-6012E's limit is 0.75 % of the diagonal for surface-mount boards — on a 200 mm board that is 1.5 mm of bow, which sounds generous until you try to place a 0.5 mm-pitch BGA on it.

Stackup presets: GND planes only

The Preset list fills the table from seven curated stackups: 4, 6, 8 and 10 layers, each in a best-SI and a high-density arrangement. The first is 4-layer SIG/GND/GND/SIG (recommended).

Every plane in them is GND, the same policy as the PCB Design Wizard:

  • power is routed on the signal layers as wide traces or local pours;
  • the GND planes are stitched together with vias;
  • every signal layer has a GND reference within 4.5 mil.

The 6-layer high-density preset (S-G-S-S-G-S) and the 8-layer best-SI preset (S-G-S-G-G-S-G-S) are the wizard's own arrangements.

The one thing given up. A tight centre pair of planes 4 mil apart is two GND planes, not GND and power. So it loses the plane capacitance a GND–power pair would add: about 240 pF/in² at 4 mil and Dk 4.3. The decoupling capacitors supply that instead.

A preset fills the rows with 1 oz copper, 90 % coverage on planes and 50 % on signal layers. Edit the coverages to match your layout before you trust the score. Watch the presets that pair a plane with a signal layer across the centre. For example, the 3-signal 6-layer preset mirrors L3 GND onto L4 signal. Those score lower until the signal layer is copper-filled.

BGA Escape Routing

Answers "how many signal layers will it take to break this BGA out", from the pad geometry and your fab's design rules.

Inputs. Ball pitch, pad diameter, the pad-to-trace clearance from your DRC, and your trace width and spacing.

Outputs. Free space between adjacent pads, channels per row (how many traces physically fit between two balls), the trace+space channel pitch, and the number of signal layers needed for a full breakout.

The geometry. Free space = pitch − pad diameter − 2 × clearance. Channels = floor((free space + space) / (width + space)). One channel per row means you escape one row per layer, which is why fine-pitch parts explode layer counts.

Insight. At 0.5 mm pitch with an 0.24 mm land and 4 mil clearance there is essentially no surface channel left — that is the point at which via-in-pad or HDI microvia escape stops being an optimisation and becomes the only option. Size the land first in the BGA Pad Designer, then bring the land diameter here.
